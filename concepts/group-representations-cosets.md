## Definition

**Group representations and cosets** are the language of group representation theory in which the generalising solution found by a network trained on a finite group's operation is described ([modular arithmetic](modular-arithmetic.md) — cyclic groups; composition of permutations — symmetric groups). The claim is that after [grokking](grokking.md) the network implements not an arbitrary fit but the structure dictated by the group's **representations**: irreducible representations (irreps — the minimal blocks into which the group's action decomposes; for abelian and cyclic groups these are one-dimensional Fourier bases) and **cosets** (shifts of a subgroup, `gH`), on which the network's neurons and circuits activate selectively \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]\[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]\[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗.

## Elaboration

Originally, the interpretability of grokking on modular addition showed that the network builds its solution out of Fourier components (frequencies) — this is the "Clock" algorithm of the [Clock and Pizza](clock-vs-pizza.md) pair; Fourier bases are exactly the irreducible representations of the cyclic group. On moving to non-abelian groups (composition of permutations in `S5`, say) continuous frequencies are no longer enough, and the network rests on discrete coset structures (coset circuits — subnetworks active on particular `gH`). The work 2505.18266 unifies both cases by introducing **approximate cosets**: it is proved that ReLU neurons on modular addition activate only on approximate cosets or on linear combinations of them, and the universal algorithm itself is read as an approximate Chinese Remainder Theorem (the decomposition of residues over coprime moduli) \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The theoretical support comes from 2509.21519: the regular representation of a group (its action on itself) decomposes into irreducible blocks, and for an abelian group all complex irreps are one-dimensional, that is, they coincide with the Fourier basis \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. The work 2601.21150 raises a neighbouring question — whether a narrow network can "pick up" group-theoretic notions at all (commutativity, the identity, subgroups) and how this can be read off its internal representations \[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗. There is a dispute around the concept over whether the continuous (Fourier/spherical) geometric prior is universal: 2603.05228 shows that for the non-abelian `S5` the discrete coset structure does not agree with a continuous spherical geometry, and that an acceleration which works on cyclic arithmetic therefore fails there \[[2.1](#ref-2-1)\]. On the other hand, 2604.00316 supports the representation-centred reading: generalisation sets in exactly when the network **recovers** the action of the symmetry group hidden in the data, and the learned feature matrices encode its specific elements \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. Here the notion is closely tied to the [phase transition](phase-transition.md) and to the network's exit from the [memorisation phase](memorization-phase.md): the replacement of the memorising solution by a representation-structured generalising one is what is observed as the jump.

## Alternative definitions and nuances

### A. The representation-theoretic reading (irreps / Fourier bases)

The generalising solution is identified with a decomposition over the group's irreducible representations. The key distinguishing mark is that the regular representation decomposes into a direct sum of irreps, and for an **abelian** group each complex irrep is one-dimensional, that is, literally a Fourier harmonic; for a non-abelian group multidimensional blocks appear and the Fourier description ceases to be complete \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. On this reading, "to learn the group" = "to learn its table of irreducible characters", and the frequencies of the Clock algorithm are the special (abelian) case.

### B. The coset-centred reading (universality through approximate cosets)

Here the unit of description is not a frequency but a coset `gH`. The distinguishing machinery is the notion of an **approximate coset**: a neuron is active on a subset of inputs close to a shift of a subgroup, and it is that subset, rather than any individual frequency, that explains the computation. The strength of the reading is its universality: one and the same coset language covers both modular addition (cyclic groups) and composition of permutations (coset circuits), tying "incredibly different groups" together by a single abstract algorithm \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The difference from version A: a coset description does not require the solution to be continuously Fourier; it covers the discrete non-abelian cases as well, where A is incomplete.

### Contested

2603.05228 contests the nuance of the **universality of the continuous geometric prior**. The distinguishing source is the contrast between cyclic arithmetic (where constraining to a sphere by `L2` normalisation removes the generalisation delay, because the solution is Fourier-continuous) and the non-abelian `S5` (where the same constraint fails completely, because the successful solutions rest on **discrete** coset structures incompatible with a spherical geometry). The conclusion: bypassing the [memorisation phase](memorization-phase.md) depends on the architectural prior being aligned with the task's intrinsic symmetry, and is not a general effect of constraining capacity \[[2.1](#ref-2-1)\].

### In support

2604.00316 joins the representation-centred reading from the side of kernel methods (RFM — the Recursive Feature Machine, which iteratively builds a feature matrix through the AGOP, the average gradient outer product). The distinguishing testable thesis is that generalisation sets in only when a certain symmetry of the training sample is **broken**, and that empirically RFM generalises precisely by **recovering** the action of the hidden symmetry group, its learned feature matrices encoding that group's specific elements \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. This carries the representation-theoretic account of grokking beyond neural networks and [SGD](optimizer-adam-adamw-sgd.md).
