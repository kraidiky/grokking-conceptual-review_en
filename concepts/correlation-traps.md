## Definition

Correlation traps are anomalously large eigenvalues (outliers) in the empirical spectral density (ESD — the histogram of a matrix's eigenvalues) of an *element-wise shuffled* layer weight matrix, which lie far beyond the upper edge of the "bulk" of the Marchenko–Pastur distribution (the limiting eigenvalue distribution for a matrix with random independent entries). Their appearance is a sign of an atypical, overfitted weight structure that impairs generalisation. The notion was introduced by Prakash and Martin in the context of [grokking](grokking.md) (delayed generalisation) as the principal diagnostic signal of the [anti-grokking](anti-grokking.md) phase — the late collapse of test accuracy \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\], \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\].

## Elaboration

The idea rests on a null hypothesis of randomness. If a layer's weight matrix is shuffled element-wise, `W → Wrand`, its entries become uncorrelated, and the ESD of such a matrix should be well described by the Marchenko–Pastur distribution. The WeightWatcher tool automatically fits an MP distribution to the "bulk" of the ESD (estimating the variance σ²) and declares a correlation trap to be any eigenvalue λ_trap that significantly (beyond Tracy–Widom fluctuations — the characteristic scale of the largest eigenvalue's fluctuations in random matrices) exceeds the upper edge of the bulk λ⁺. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov criterion is applied in addition, measuring the discrepancy between the ESD and its best MP fit: *"anomalously large eigenvalues beyond the Marchenko–Pastur bulk in the empirical spectral density of shuffled weight matrices, which are predicted to impair generalization"* \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\].

Mechanistically the traps are read as anomalous rank-one (or higher) perturbations of the weight matrix that cause a large shift in the mean of its entries, `E[Wij] → large`; this shift distorts the ESD and pushes it into the very-heavy-tailed (VHT) region, where the power-law tail exponent α falls below 2 (in [HTSR](heavy-tailed-self-regularization-htsr.md) theory — Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization — the optimum of generalisation corresponds to α ≈ 2). It is exactly the combination "numerous correlation traps + α < 2" that serves as an unambiguous indicator that the model has gone into anti-grokking \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\], \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. The appearance of traps is tied to two forms of late overfitting: in an MLP on MNIST the layers begin to encode specific prototypes of training examples (a passage from the [memorisation](memorization-phase.md) phase to prototype memorisation), while in a transformer on [modular addition](modular-arithmetic.md) the same traps cause [catastrophic forgetting](catastrophic-forgetting.md) of the generalising rule already found: *"in anti-grokking, numerous Correlation Traps also appear, but here causing catastrophic forgetting"* \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The traps are a controllable effect: in the MLP model non-zero [weight decay](weight-decay.md) suppresses their onset and reduces the depth of the dip in test accuracy \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\].

## Alternative definitions and nuances

### A. The spectral-statistical reading (SETOL / Marchenko–Pastur)

Here a trap is defined purely spectrally: it is an eigenvalue of the randomised matrix lying beyond the edge of the MP bulk, `λtrap ≫ λ+rand`. The key source of difference from an ordinary outlier is the randomisation: first all inter-element correlations are destroyed by shuffling, and only a peak that *survives* the shuffling counts as a trap. The [order parameter](order-parameter.md) is the number of traps per layer itself: it is zero in the pre-grokking and the grokking phases proper and jumps at the entry into anti-grokking, which makes it a diagnostic signal in its own right, requiring no access to test or training data. The introducing work states this as the principal signal: *"the strongest signal we have for anti-grokking is the sudden onset of Correlation Traps in both layers"* \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\].

### B. The mechanistic reading (a rank-one perturbation and a mean shift)

The alternative, mechanistic formulation explains *why* a trap harms generalisation, not only *how* to detect it. A trap is a rank-one (or higher) shift in the weight matrix that stops the distribution of entries from being centred (`E[Wij]` becomes large): the weight distribution becomes atypical, different random samples of the weights give very different means, and, like any statistical estimator with an atypical distribution, the layer stops generalising. On this reading what is primary is not the spectral outlier itself but the distortion of the distribution of weight entries: *"the 'traps' are anomalous rank-one (or greater) perturbations in the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$, causing a large mean-shift in the distribution of elements"* \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The earlier work leaves the concrete mechanism of overfitting unspecified, stating it as a hypothesis: layers with many correlation traps are conjectured to be overfitted to the training data in some unspecified way and to harm the model's overall test accuracy \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\].
