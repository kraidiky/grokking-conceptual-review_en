## Definition

**Modular arithmetic** is a class of algorithmic tasks of the form (a + b) mod p (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division modulo a number p) on which [grokking](grokking.md) was first observed and which became the canonical testbed for studying it. The primality of the modulus is a requirement of division alone, not of the notion: division is defined as the inverse of multiplication and is unique only for prime p (for a composite modulus the equation $b\cdot y\equiv a\bmod p$ has zero or several solutions, and a division dataset would contain identical inputs with different answers); for addition, subtraction and multiplication any integer p will do — Gromov explicitly allows composite moduli for his class of total functions \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\], Power's own equivalence argument (§3.2) goes through subtraction mod 96, and Lyle et al. set up addition modulo the composite 117 \[[4.36](#ref-4-36)\]. Power et al. (2022) introduced it as a test environment: the model was trained on "the binary operation of division mod 97" using only half the data \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. Gromov (2023) gave the first analytical treatment of how a simple network masters modular arithmetic tasks \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. The experimental frame in which these tasks are presented to a network — the encoding by uninformative symbols, the random split of the table, the small transformer — is set out in a separate card: the [canonical grokking testbed](canonical-grokking-testbed.md).

## Elaboration

Modular arithmetic was originally introduced as an *algorithmic dataset*: pairs of residues modulo a prime p are fed in as separate symbols, and the network must predict the result of a binary operation — for example, division modulo 97 \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The task is convenient in that it is small, fully observable and admits an analytical treatment of the training dynamics \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. The canonical mechanistic description was given by Nanda et al.: a network trained on modular addition implements an algorithm based on the discrete Fourier transform and trigonometric identities, converting addition into a rotation about a circle \[[1.3](#ref-1-3)\] (the competing algorithmic solutions of this task are ["clock vs pizza"](clock-vs-pizza.md)). Formally the task is written as an operation over the ring of residues Zp, (a + b) mod p \[[1.4](#ref-1-4)\]. From a group-theoretic point of view modular addition is an abelian (commutative) group, and the natural representation for it is a Fourier basis \[[3.5](#ref-3-5)\]; networks pick up other group-theoretic properties as well — the identity element, commutativity, subgroups \[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗ — and before grokking they struggle to master even the commutative law \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. The task is not limited to addition: it is extended to multiplication, subtraction and division, and to polynomial and composite ("complex") modular operations \[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\].

Modular arithmetic serves as the standard testbed on which general claims about grokking are checked and contested. Classically one observes on it a long [memorisation phase](memorization-phase.md) with a plateau of test accuracy, giving way to an abrupt [phase transition](phase-transition.md) to generalisation; it was on modular addition that it was also used for a unifying view of grokking, [double descent](double-descent.md) and [emergent abilities](emergence.md). A number of works show, however, that neither the "suddenness" of the transition nor the plateau itself is an inherent property of the task: tuning the laziness parameter (a measure of how far the network stays in the almost linear, kernel regime) makes grokking on modular addition vanish continuously \[[2.1](#ref-2-1)\], and networks with a bounded (cosine) output geometry learn modular addition and multiplication with no separable memorisation phase at all \[[2.2](#ref-2-2)\]. The source of the task's difficulty is also under discussion: it is linked to the symmetries of the addition table \[[3.3](#ref-3-3)\] and to the intrinsic symmetry of the task \[[3.7](#ref-3-7)\], while generalisation is linked to the need to break the symmetry of the data in the setting of filling in a Cayley table (the multiplication table of a group) \[[3.8](#ref-3-8)\]. Part of the literature, meanwhile, defends universality: the disparate neural solutions of modular addition reduce to a single abstract algorithm \[[3.4](#ref-3-4)\].

## Alternative definitions and nuances

### A. The canonical algorithmic testbed (the "fruit fly" of grokking)

The base reading of Power et al.: modular arithmetic is a set of algorithmic tasks (a binary operation over residues modulo a prime) on which delayed generalisation was first shown \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. Its smallness, full observability and the demand for exact generalisation make it a reference controllable testbed; Gromov carried it through to an analytically solvable model with vanilla gradient descent and no regularisation \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. The unit of the reading here is the setting of the task as a test environment, not the mechanism of its solution.

### B. The group-theoretic and Fourier structure

Here modular arithmetic is defined not operationally but through algebra: modular addition is an abelian group (the ring of residues Zp), and the optimal representations for it are Fourier bases \[[3.5](#ref-3-5)\]. The canonical solution found by a network consists of trigonometric identities converting addition into a rotation about a circle \[[1.3](#ref-1-3)\]; the network picks up the identity element, commutativity and subgroups \[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗ and only with difficulty masters the commutative law before grokking \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. The key difference of this reading: the unit of difference is the algebraic structure of the task (the symmetries of the group), not the optimisation dynamics of training.

### C. The extension beyond modular addition

A definition that lifts the tie to addition: modular arithmetic includes modular multiplication, subtraction and division, as well as polynomial and composite operations over Zp \[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\]. The key difference from readings A/B: what changes is the set of operations and their Fourier signatures (polynomials give a superposition of the frequencies of the elementary operations), which extends the test surface beyond a single addition operation.

### D. The universality of the abstract algorithm

A reading through an invariant of the solution: despite the variability of the neural representations, different networks (MLPs and transformers) on modular addition implement one and the same abstract algorithm \[[3.4](#ref-3-4)\]. The difference from reading B: the unit of analysis is not a concrete scheme of neurons but an architecture-invariant abstract algorithm over a group; the variations at the level of neurons are read as different realisations of one solution rather than as different algorithms.

### Contested

What is contested is that the classical signs of grokking — the memorisation plateau and the discontinuity of the transition — are inherent properties of modular arithmetic. Kumar et al.: merely tuning the laziness parameter (the output scale of the network) is enough to make grokking on modular addition vanish continuously — so the "suddenness" is not a property of the task \[[2.1](#ref-2-1)\]. Yildirim: with a bounded cosine geometry of the output layer, a network on modular addition and multiplication learns with no separable memorisation phase at all \[[2.2](#ref-2-2)\]. The source of difference here is the control parameter (the laziness or the geometry of the output head), not the arithmetic itself.

### In support

Joining the structural/symmetry reading of the task: generalisation on modular arithmetic is linked to the symmetries of the addition table \[[3.3](#ref-3-3)\], to the intrinsic symmetry of the task, carried over to a wider class of algebraic domains \[[3.7](#ref-3-7)\], and to the need to break the symmetry of the data when filling in a Cayley table \[[3.8](#ref-3-8)\]. The common thesis: what makes modular arithmetic solvable through grokking is its algebraic symmetry rather than particular details of the training procedure.
