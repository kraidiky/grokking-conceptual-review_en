## Definition

**Double descent** is a non-monotone behaviour of the test error: as the model size (or the amount of data, or the number of training epochs) grows, the error first falls, then rises to a peak near the **interpolation point** (where the model fits all the training examples exactly), and in the overparameterised region falls **again** — hence the "double" descent \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. It was introduced by Belkin et al. (2019) and Nakkiran et al.; in work on [grokking](grokking.md) it is discussed as a phenomenon akin to delayed generalisation.

## Elaboration

Double descent appears in several forms. **Model-wise** (in model size) — the classical Belkin/Nakkiran curve: the error rises to a peak at the interpolation point and falls again in the overparameterised regime \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. **Epoch-wise** (in the number of training epochs) — the same non-monotonicity but in time: the validation loss first falls, then rises, then falls sharply; it is this form that makes double descent akin to grokking \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]\[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. There are narrower versions too — for instance a **double descent of feature ranks** (the number of linearly independent directions in a network's activations) \[[1.3](#ref-1-3)\].

The main question is **whether this is one phenomenon with grokking**. Some unify them: grokking, double descent and [emergence](emergence.md) are derived from a common frame of **circuit competition** — a memorising circuit (a subnetwork storing examples) against a generalising one (implementing the algorithm), that is, from [circuit efficiency](circuit-efficiency.md), with the [critical data size](data-fraction-critical-dataset-size.md) or model size governing both \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. They are also read as consequences of a single "pattern-learning" model \[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\]. Others — beginning with the original paper on grokking — hold that grokking **may be distinct** from double descent: here the second descent is visible only in the **loss, not in accuracy**, and comes **tens of thousands of epochs later** than the moment the training loss became small (in classical double descent it follows immediately after the interpolation point) \[[2.1](#ref-2-1)\].

## Alternative definitions and nuances

### A. Model-wise (in model size)

The classical Belkin/Nakkiran form: the test error as a function of **model size** — a fall, a peak at the interpolation point, a second fall in the overparameterised region \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. The source of the difference: the control parameter is the size (capacity) of the model.

### B. Epoch-wise (in training time)

Non-monotonicity along the axis of **epochs**: the validation loss falls → rises → falls sharply. It is this form that is closest to grokking (the same "hump" of error in training time) \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]\[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. The source of the difference: the control parameter is the number of epochs, not the model size.

### C. Generalised forms (feature ranks)

Double descent occurs not only in the error: **feature ranks** (the number of independent directions in the activations) show the same double course \[[1.3](#ref-1-3)\]. The source of the difference: double descent as a property of an internal quantity of the network, not only of an external performance metric.

### Contested

- **Grokking ≠ double descent** \[[2.1](#ref-2-1)\]: the original paper on grokking conjectures that these may be different phenomena and names two concrete differences — (1) the non-monotonicity here is only in the **loss, not in accuracy** (classical double descent is non-monotone precisely in the error/accuracy); (2) the second descent comes **tens of thousands of epochs later** than the point at which the training loss had already become small (in the classical variant it follows immediately after the interpolation point). The source of the difference: a common mechanism is denied — both the timing and the quantity in which the non-monotonicity appears diverge.

### In support

- **A single mechanism** \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]\[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\]: grokking and double descent (and with them emergence) are derived from one frame — the competition of memorising and generalising circuits \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\], or a single "pattern-learning" model \[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\]. The source of the difference: a common cause is postulated, with the critical data or model size serving as a shared control parameter.
