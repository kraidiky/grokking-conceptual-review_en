## Definition

**Generalisation bounds** are provable (formally derivable) upper or lower estimates on a model's ability to generalise: on the **generalisation error** (the gap between the training and the population error, that is, the error averaged over the whole data distribution), on the **sample complexity** (the number of examples needed for the model to start generalising) or — as applied to [grokking](grokking.md) — on the **length of the delay** before generalisation sets in. In the context of grokking such bounds turn the empirical observation of delayed generalisation into a quantitative theory: they formalise under what conditions and how quickly generalisation arrives. The first rigorous estimates for this regime are introduced in the analysis of [modular addition](modular-arithmetic.md), where lower bounds on the population loss are proved \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\], and are then extended to the PAC-Bayes \[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗, Bayesian \[[1.6](#ref-1-6)\] and information-geometric \[[1.5](#ref-1-5)\] families of estimates.

## Elaboration

The generalisation bounds in the grokking corpus fall into several branches, differing in which quantity is bounded and through which **complexity parameter** it is controlled. The first branch bounds **the generalisation error or the sample complexity itself**. Mohamadi et al. prove lower bounds on the population ℓ2 loss (the expected squared error over the whole distribution) for learning modular addition with kernel predictors, and show that a small ℓ∞ norm allows generalisation from substantially fewer examples \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]; the key role here is played by the choice of **complexity measure** (ℓ2 against ℓ∞ norm, the intrinsic dimension of the predictor). Rubin et al. formulate a grokking-based mechanism that lowers the sample complexity relative to the Gaussian-process limit (the GP limit — the "lazy" training regime in which the network is equivalent to a fixed kernel and learns no features) \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The second branch builds estimates through specialised complexity parameters: a PAC-Bayes bound (a class of probabilistic estimates in which the gap is controlled by the KL divergence of the posterior over weights from the prior), which reveals which metrics of the network affect generalisation \[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗; an upper bound on the generalisation error through the population within-class variance of the representations (linked to [neural collapse](neural-collapse.md) — the contraction of a class's representations to a point) \[[1.5](#ref-1-5)\]; an estimate within singular learning theory (SLT), where the local learning coefficient λ (a coordinate-invariant measure of a minimum's local statistical complexity) governs the asymptotic Bayesian generalisation error \[[1.6](#ref-1-6)\]; and a bound for a mixture-of-experts architecture (networks that route the input among "experts"), improved by more structured routing pathways \[[1.4](#ref-1-4)\].

A separate branch, specific to grokking, bounds not the error but the **length of the delay** between the [memorisation phase](memorization-phase.md) and the onset of generalisation. Xu et al. give the first rigorous quantitative bounds on the "grokking time" in terms of training hyperparameters, showing that the delay can be amplified or removed by tuning them \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]; Truong et al. prove matching upper and lower bounds on a delay growing logarithmically with the ratio of the competing solutions' norms \[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\]. Both works draw the same conclusion from this: since the delay admits rigorous bounds, grokking is not a mysterious failure but a predictable consequence of the training conditions. Bounds on the delay thereby bring grokking closer to the [phase transition](phase-transition.md) reading, but move it from a qualitative description to a quantitative law.

## Alternative definitions and nuances

### A. Bounds on the generalisation error and the sample complexity

The classical reading: a generalisation bound estimates the gap between the training and the population error, or the number of examples needed to generalise. The distinguishing machinery is the **complexity measure** that enters the estimate. In Mohamadi et al. it is the ℓ∞ norm (against ℓ2), under which the network provably generalises from far fewer examples than the kernel regime requires; the proof technique analyses the population ℓ2 loss of function classes through the intrinsic dimension of the predictor \[[1.2](#ref-1-2)\]. In Rubin et al. the parameter is the comparison with the GP limit: the grokking mechanism lowers the sample complexity relative to the lazy kernel regime by means of [feature learning](feature-emergence-feature-learning.md) \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\].

### B. Information-theoretic, Bayesian and geometric estimates

Here the bound is defined not through the weight norm but through another [order parameter](order-parameter.md), read off the internal structure or the posterior geometry. A PAC-Bayes estimate controls the gap through the KL divergence of the weight posterior and links generalisation to specific metrics of the network \[[5.1](#ref-5-1)\]↗. In SLT the controlling quantity is the local learning coefficient λ, which sets the asymptotic Bayesian generalisation error, so that simpler minima (smaller λ) are statistically preferred \[[1.6](#ref-1-6)\]. In Sakamoto et al. the parameter is the population within-class variance of the representations: its contraction yields an upper bound on the generalisation error \[[1.5](#ref-1-5)\]. In Li et al.'s analysis of a single-layer MoE the bound is improved by the structuredness of the routing pathways \[[1.4](#ref-1-4)\].

### In support

Works joining the nuance "rigorous bounds are placed not on the error but on the delay of generalisation itself, which makes grokking predictable and controllable". Xu et al. give the first quantitative bounds on the "grokking time" through hyperparameters, showing explicitly that the delay can be amplified or zeroed by tuning \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. Truong et al. prove tight upper and lower bounds on a delay growing logarithmically with the ratio of the memorising and the generalising solutions' norms \[[3.2](#ref-3-2)\]. Both bound one and the same grokking-specific quantity — the interval between memorisation and generalisation, not the population risk.
