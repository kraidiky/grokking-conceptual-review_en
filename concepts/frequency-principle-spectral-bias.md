## Definition

**The frequency principle** (F-Principle), also known as **spectral bias**, is an implicit inductive bias of neural networks whereby they fit the target function "from low frequencies to high": slowly varying (low-frequency) components of the signal are learned first, and high-frequency detail substantially later \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The notion itself was introduced outside the grokking corpus — as the "frequency principle (F-Principle)" by Xu et al. (2019, 2020, 2022) and as "spectral bias" by Rahaman et al. (2019); into the context of [grokking](grokking.md) this optics was brought by Zhou et al. (2024), who accounted for delayed generalisation through the frequency dynamics of training: at the early stage the network learns the frequency components that are less salient in the test data, and that is what produces the delay \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\].

## Elaboration

"Frequency" here means frequency in the spectrum (the Fourier decomposition) of the target function over the input, not "frequency of occurrence"; "low frequencies" are smooth, global regularities, "high" ones are sharp, local detail. The key observation of the F-principle is that the decay rate of the loss in the frequency domain is set by the smoothness (regularity) of the activation functions, so that under ordinary (small) initialisation the network converges on the low frequencies first \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. Zhou et al. tie this to grokking as follows: under insufficient and uneven sampling, a spurious low-frequency component appears in the spectrum of the training data (an aliasing effect — a high frequency "masquerading" as a low one because of sparse discretisation) that is not present in the spectrum of the test data; following the F-principle, the network first faithfully fits this parasitic low frequency — the training loss falls while the test loss rises (this corresponds to the [memorisation phase](memorization-phase.md)) — and only later, having learned the true high frequencies, does it generalise \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The sharp jump of the test curve at that moment makes the picture akin to the reading of grokking as a [phase transition](phase-transition.md). An important control parameter is the scale of weight initialisation: at large initialisation the F-principle ceases to hold, the network fits all frequencies at roughly the same time, and the mechanism of the delay changes (shown by Zhou et al. on MNIST with large initialisation) \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. A separate (more theoretical) line explains spectral bias through the spectrum of the [neural tangent kernel](neural-tangent-kernel-ntk.md) (NTK — a linearisation of the network around its initial weights, under which training reduces to kernel regression): the convergence rate of each error mode is proportional to the corresponding eigenvalue of the kernel, and since only a few eigenvalues turn out to be "large", most (high-frequency) modes converge slowly \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. From the same optics follows the tie between spectral bias and the lazy (NTK / kernel) training regime: a protracted stay in it is exactly the source of the delay before generalisation \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\].

## Alternative definitions and nuances

### A. A train/test frequency mismatch as the cause of grokking (Zhou et al.)

A definition through a spectral mismatch of the samples: grokking arises not from regularisation or adaptive [optimisers](optimizer-adam-adamw-sgd.md) but from a disagreement between the spectra of the training and the test data, produced by insufficient and uneven sampling \[[1.1](#ref-1-1)\]. The [order parameter](order-parameter.md) here is the divergence between the frequency "preferred" by the training dynamics and the frequency dominant in the test data; the control levers are the density and evenness of the sampling (with sufficient or uniform sampling the spectra agree and grokking disappears) and the initialisation scale (which switches whether the F-principle holds). The key difference from accounts through weights and the optimiser: the mechanism requires neither constraints on the dimensionality of the data nor explicit regularisation, and rests on the frequency dynamics alone; the authors state explicitly that on language (algorithmic) data the mechanism may not work.

### B. Spectral bias through the eigenspectrum of the NTK (Jiang et al.)

A definition through the linear algebra of the kernel: spectral bias is a direct consequence of the evolution of the error along the eigenmodes of the NTK, where a mode with eigenvalue λ decays at a rate set by that λ, and global convergence is governed by the condition number of the kernel \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. The difference from the frame of Zhou et al.: the source of the difference is moved from the distribution of the data to the spectrum of the tangent kernel, and the control lever is a gradient preconditioner (preconditioned GD, Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt style methods): by levelling the eigenvalues it "erases" the spectral bias, allows the frequency modes to be explored evenly, and thereby shortens the grokking delay. Spectral bias thus turns from a purely descriptive fact into a quantity an optimiser can be aimed at.

### In support

Jiang et al. join the spectral reading of grokking and strengthen it: both the frame of Zhou et al. (frequency mismatch) and the kindred frame of Kumar et al. (a protracted lazy regime) come down to the claim that "grokking stems from spectral bias combined with prolonged confinement to the lazy training regime" \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\]. Their own contribution is empirical and theoretical evidence: preconditioned gradient descent, by removing spectral bias in the NTK regime, uniformly shortens the delay before generalisation on a range of tasks ([modular arithmetic](modular-arithmetic.md), polynomial regression, MNIST), whence the conclusion that the leading role is played neither by overfitting nor by adaptivity but precisely by spectral bias \[[3.1](#ref-3-1)\].
