## Concepts covered

**[Grokking](../../concepts/grokking.md)** — the work introduces the notion and gives it its name. The term appears in the list of contributions ([`p2-4`](#p2-3)) as a name for an observation: long after severe overfitting, validation accuracy sometimes suddenly begins to rise from the level of chance to perfect generalisation. The defining illustration is [`fig-1`](#fig-1): division mod 97, with training accuracy near perfect within $<10^{3}$ steps and validation accuracy reaching the same level at around $10^{6}$ steps. A detail of the setup that gets lost in retellings: this figure is obtained on Adam **without weight decay** at a budget of $10^{6}$ steps ([A.1.2](#sec-a1-2)), whereas the main body of the work — including the conclusion about the effectiveness of weight decay — runs on AdamW with a weight decay of 1 and a budget of $10^{5}$. That is, the canonical grokking plot demonstrates not the effect of weight decay but the scale of the delay in its absence. The work gave the phenomenon an empirical rather than a mechanistic frame: it asserts no discontinuity of the transition, introduces no progress measures and proposes no mechanism — all of that appears in later authors, and in the concept card it comes from them rather than from here.

**[Modular arithmetic](../../concepts/modular-arithmetic.md)** — the work makes modular arithmetic the standard proving ground for the study of delayed generalisation. The canonical setup became division mod 97 at 50 % of the data in the training set, in which each of the 97 residues is fed to the network as a separate uninformative symbol ([`fig-1`](#fig-1)). The full list of 12 operations — 9 modular ones at $p=97$ and 3 on $S_{5}$ — is set out in [appendix A.1.1](#sec-a1-1); it is precisely this set of tasks that a substantial part of the corpus's subsequent works inherited, down to the particular choice of $p=97$.

**[The canonical grokking proving ground](../../concepts/canonical-grokking-testbed.md)** — the work sets out the proving ground in full rather than the task alone: binary operation tables as equations of five uninformative tokens ([`sec-2`](#sec-2)), a list of 12 operations ([A.1.1](#sec-a1-1)), a random fraction of the table as the chief control parameter, and a small decoder transformer with a tuned optimisation ([A.1.2](#sec-a1-2)). It is this frame that the corpus inherited: "following Power et al." in later works means a reproduction of the encoding, the split and the architecture, with pointwise variations (the modulus, the loss, the model). The equivalence of operations from [§3.2](#sec-3-2) rests on the uninformativeness of the symbols too: $x+y\pmod{p-1}$ and $x * y\pmod{p}$ are indistinguishable to the network precisely because tables can be told apart only by the structure of the symbols' interactions. The composition of the frame and the variations of its inheritors are analysed in the concept card.

**[The memorisation phase](../../concepts/memorization-phase.md)** — the work formulates its subject in such a way that memorisation becomes the reference point: generalisation is studied "beyond the memorisation of a finite training set" ([`p1-1`](#p1-4)). Memorisation here, though, is a state the network leaves rather than a phase with an internal structure of its own: the work contains no notion of a memorising solution being displaced by a generalising one, and the concept receives from it only the frame "first it memorised, then it generalised".

**[The data fraction / the critical dataset size](../../concepts/data-fraction-critical-dataset-size.md)** — the work gives the first quantitative observation of how the volume of data governs the time to generalisation: smaller datasets require an ever greater amount of optimisation ([`p1-1`](#p1-4)). The measure of the effect is in [§3.1.1](#sec-3-1-1): in the neighbourhood of 25–30 % of the data a reduction of the training set by 1 % gives a rise of 40–50 % in the median time to generalisation, whereas the number of steps to 99 % training accuracy falls over that same range. The work introduces no threshold language: the term "critical dataset size" appears later, in Liu et al., as an interpretation of this same curve.

**[Phase transition](../../concepts/phase-transition.md)** — the work supplies the notion with its original observation but not with an interpretation. The formula "suddenly begins to rise" ([`p2-4`](#p2-3)) became the fact that subsequent works described in the language of phase transitions; in Power et al. themselves there are no order parameters, no non-analyticity and no use of the word "transition" in that sense, and so in the concept card the work appears under "Citations" rather than as the source of the definition.

**[The emergence of features](../../concepts/feature-emergence-feature-learning.md)** — the work shows for the first time that in a generalised network the structure of the mathematical objects themselves shows through in the embeddings ([`p2-4`](#p2-3)). This is developed in [§3.4](#sec-3-4) and in the caption to figure 3: a t-SNE of the output layer gives the cosets of a subgroup for $S_{5}$ and a circular "number line" for modular addition, with the structure more distinct under training with weight decay. The contribution remains qualitative — the authors themselves call the section a "qualitative visualisation" and propose no measure of feature emergence. The weight of these visualisations is set by the uninformativeness of the symbols: the circular topology of addition and the cosets of the subgroup are not given to the network at the input and are recovered from co-occurrence statistics alone — which is why §3.4 shows more than "clusters are visible in the plot" (see the card on [the canonical proving ground](../../concepts/canonical-grokking-testbed.md)).

**[The optimiser](../../concepts/optimizer-adam-adamw-sgd.md)** — the work raises for the first time the question of how the details of the optimisation affect data efficiency, and answers it by a sweep ([`p2-4`](#p2-3)). The set of compared variants, set out in [appendix A.1.2](#sec-a1-2), became a model for subsequent ablations: full-batch Adam, ordinary Adam, Adam with Gaussian noise in the update direction, Adam with a residual dropout of 0.1, AdamW with a weight decay of 1, AdamW with weight decay towards the initialisation, Adam at learning rates $3\cdot10^{-4}$ and $3\cdot10^{-3}$, and Adam with Gaussian noise in the weights of 0.01.

**[Weight decay](../../concepts/weight-decay.md)** — the work establishes weight decay as the most effective of the interventions tested ([`p2-4`](#p2-3)) and gives this a measure in [§3.3](#sec-3-3): it "more than halves the number of examples required in comparison with most of the other interventions". In the same place stands the authors' caveat, which limits the explanation: weight decay towards the initialisation also works, but less well than towards the origin, and so a prior of approximately zero weights explains "part but not all" of the advantage. It is precisely this caveat that left room for the later explanations through the weight norm.

**[Group composition / S5](../../concepts/group-composition-non-commutative-s5.md)** — the work brings a non-commutative task into circulation: the product in the abstract group $S_{5}$ ([`fig-2`](#fig-2)), which became the standard counterexample to "grokking is about modular arithmetic". The related operations $x\cdot y\cdot x^{-1}$ and $x\cdot y\cdot x$ are set out in [A.1.1](#sec-a1-1), and the caption to figure 3 adds to the task the first structural observation — the embedding clusters turn out to be the cosets of a subgroup.

**[Generalisation bounds](../../concepts/generalization-bounds.md)** — the work touches on the theme twice and in different ways. In the survey ([`p7-6`](#p7-6)) it merely mentions sample complexity on algorithmic tasks — and it is for this fragment that the card cites it, under "Citations". The substantive contribution, however, lies in [A.5](#sec-a5): the sharpness of the minimum by Keskar gives a Spearman correlation of $-0.79548$ with validation accuracy, which became the first indication of a connection between generalisation measures and grokking. The authors themselves call this a preliminary investigation on a single dataset.

**[Double descent](../../concepts/double-descent.md)** — the work draws a distinction that the notion still uses today ([`p9-2`](#p9-2)): the second descent of the validation loss they observe may, in the authors' view, differ from the double descent of Nakkiran/Belkin, because it occurs much later than the moment at which the training loss first becomes very small and is not accompanied by a non-monotonicity of the accuracy. The distinction is offered as a hypothesis rather than an established fact, and in the concept card it occupies the position of the contesting side.

The cards of the corpus's remaining works are listed in the [index of papers](../index.md). The works citing Power et al. 2022 are collected in an external citation table (not stored in this repository); their texts have not been copied here — the corpus refers to the cards' own `original/`.

**[The role of gradient noise](../../concepts/gradient-noise.md)** — the work at once opens and delimits the noise line: the hypothesis that grokking "might be driven by noise from SGD" ([A.3](#sec-a3)); two Gaussian noise interventions in the sweep of [A.1.2](#sec-a1-2) that improve data efficiency but fall short of weight decay; and a full-batch ablation in which the generalisation occurs with no noise whatever ([`fig-2`](#fig-2)). The batch size of the main series is min(512, half the training set), so the canonical runs are nearly noiseless.

**[When regularisation is necessary](../../concepts/regularization-necessity.md)** — the work lays the groundwork for both sides of the future dispute: weight decay is established as the most effective of the interventions ([`p2-4`](#p2-3)), yet the canonical run is made [with none of it at all](#sec-a1-2) ([`fig-1`](#fig-1)) — the generalisation sets in even so, only much later. In the card on the dispute the work stands as the primary source of the question.

**[The intrinsic symmetry of the task](../../concepts/intrinsic-task-symmetry.md)** — an observation out of which, four years later, a causal claim grew: operations symmetric in the order of their operands require less data than closely related asymmetric ones ([`p3-4`](#p3-4)). The work itself is cautious and allows that the effect may be partly architectural.

**[Grokking time](../../concepts/grokking-time.md)** — the delay is named as the subject of study from the very beginning: small algorithmic datasets are chosen because on them generalisation and the speed of learning can be analysed in great detail ([`p1-4`](#p1-4)).
## Points we could not reconcile

These are the card editor's remarks, not the authors' text. The point is not disputable interpretations but places where two fragments of the paper itself say different things. All three matter when citing: retellings usually pick up the short formulation from the main text, while the qualification stays in a figure caption or an appendix.

**The delay factor: 1000× or 100×.** [§3.1](#sec-3-1) says that validation accuracy begins to rise above the chance level "only after 1000× more optimisation steps than are required for training accuracy to approach the optimum". The caption to figure 1 ([`fig-1`](#fig-1)) gives different numbers: training accuracy is near perfect within $<10^{3}$ steps, "up to $10^{5}$ steps we see almost no evidence of any generalisation", and validation reaches the training level at around $10^{6}$. Measured by the onset of the rise, the ratio is $10^{5}/10^{3}=100$. The factor of 1000 comes out only when comparing the moment at which training reached the optimum with the moment at which validation caught up. In citing "1000×", one must state that the reference is to the completion of the grokking rather than to its onset.

**"Typical of all the operations" against the operations that did not grok.** [§3.1](#sec-3-1) asserts that the behaviour described is "typical of all the binary operations at dataset sizes close to the minimum at which the network generalised within the budget allotted". [§3.2](#sec-3-2) reports that a number of the operations (for instance $x^{3}+xy^{2}+y\pmod{97}$) did not generalise at any fraction of the data up to 95 %. For them no minimum size exists, that is, "all" silently excludes them. Formally the qualification saves the claim, but the sentence from §3.1, taken on its own, is stronger than what is shown.

**The list of operations does not cover figure 2.** [Appendix A.1.1](#sec-a1-1) lists 12 operations and contains neither $x * y\pmod{p}$ nor any operation modulo $p-1$ — only $x+y\pmod{p}$. Yet [§3.2](#sec-3-2) analyses the equivalence of $x+y\pmod{p-1}$ and $x * y\pmod{p}$ and then names $x * y$ among the symmetric operations "listed in figure 2 (right)" ([`fig-2`](#fig-2)). So either the list of A.1.1 is incomplete, or figure 2 contains operations outside it. This matters because it is precisely A.1.1 that subsequent works reproduce as "the task set of Power et al.".

## Common misreadings and overstatements of this paper

Patterns of misreading or overstatement of this work, as observed in the citing papers of the corpus. Each entry says what exactly gets distorted in the restatement and leads to the authors' own paragraphs, where the qualification that goes missing still stands. The specific wordings are examined in the citing works' cards, in their "Common misreadings and overstatements" sections, where the "details" links lead.

**"Weight decay is necessary for grokking" (erroneous).** The work is credited with the claim that without weight decay grokking does not occur at all — that test accuracy without it never rises. Power et al. wrote nothing of the kind, and their own canonical plot refutes it: the run of figure 1 is made [on Adam with no weight decay at all](#sec-a1-2), and validation accuracy on it nonetheless reaches the training level — merely after $\sim 10^{6}$ steps. The work's actual claim is more modest and concerns the economy of data: [weight decay is "particularly effective at improving generalisation on the tasks we study"](#p2-3) — it accelerates and eases the generalisation but is not its condition. Details: [2310.19470](../2310.19470.bridging-lottery-ticket-and-grokking-understanding-grokking-from-inner-structure-of-networks/2310.19470.bridging-lottery-ticket-and-grokking-understanding-grokking-from-inner-structure-of-networks.card.md#mc-2201-02177).

**"Weight decay causes grokking" (widening, explicitly refuted).** A milder form of the same drift: weight decay is called a precondition of grokking or "one of the factors causing" it, with a reference to this work. In the authors themselves the grokking of figure 1 occurs with no weight decay whatever, and within the corpus the thesis is refuted directly too: [Thilak et al.](../2206.04817.the-slingshot-mechanism-an-empirical-study-of-adaptive-optimizers-and-grokking/2206.04817.the-slingshot-mechanism-an-empirical-study-of-adaptive-optimizers-and-grokking.card.md) observe grokking with no explicit regularisation at all (the slingshot mechanism), and [Gromov](../2301.02679.grokking-modular-arithmetic/2301.02679.grokking-modular-arithmetic.card.md) on vanilla gradient descent with no regularisation. A model of accurate formulation is given by Varma et al. (2309.02390): "grokking has been observed even when weight decay is not present … (though it is slower and often much harder to elicit …)" — the fact is named together with the countervailing caveat and its source. Details: [2402.16726](../2402.16726.towards-empirical-interpretation-of-internal-circuits-and-properties-in-grokked-transformers-on-modular-polynomials/2402.16726.towards-empirical-interpretation-of-internal-circuits-and-properties-in-grokked-transformers-on-modular-polynomials.card.md#mc-2201-02177), [2310.19470](../2310.19470.bridging-lottery-ticket-and-grokking-understanding-grokking-from-inner-structure-of-networks/2310.19470.bridging-lottery-ticket-and-grokking-understanding-grokking-from-inner-structure-of-networks.card.md#mc-2201-02177).

**A conflation of the two regimes of the work (the background of most retellings).** The canonical plot and the conclusion about weight decay are usually cited as one whole, but in the paper itself they are obtained in different regimes. Figure 1 is Adam with no weight decay and a deliberately stretched budget of $10^{6}$ steps: the authors write outright that it is done that way ["to emphasise how late … generalisation may begin"](#sec-a1-2). The conclusion about the effectiveness of weight decay, meanwhile, comes from the main experimental series — AdamW with a weight decay of 1 and a budget of $10^{5}$ steps. A retelling that glues these regimes together — "in Power, at a weight decay of 1, generalisation sets in after $10^{6}$ steps" — describes a setting that does not exist in the work. An observed consequence: the hyperparameter table of 2402.16726, with its reference "following Power et al.", combines "Weight Decay 1.0" with a budget of 1e6. Details: [2402.16726](../2402.16726.towards-empirical-interpretation-of-internal-circuits-and-properties-in-grokked-transformers-on-modular-polynomials/2402.16726.towards-empirical-interpretation-of-internal-circuits-and-properties-in-grokked-transformers-on-modular-polynomials.card.md#mc-2201-02177).

**"Grokking is generalisation setting in long after the training set has been learnt" (widening, explicitly refuted — Omnigrok, 2210.01117).** Citing works write a large delay into the very definition of the phenomenon with a reference to this paper — as an unconditional, distinguishing property: generalisation "sets in only much later" than the overfitting, up to a universal factor of "more than ten times the number of iterations". In the authors the delay is conditioned by the setting. In the list of contributions [validation accuracy only "sometimes" suddenly begins to rise after the overfitting](#p2-3); [the effect is called pronounced "in some cases", and at larger dataset sizes the training and validation curves follow each other more closely](#sec-3-1); [the time to generalisation grows rapidly as the fraction of training data falls — by 40–50 % for each percent removed in the neighbourhood of 25–30 % of the data](#sec-3-1-1) (see also [the centre panel of figure 1](#fig-1)); and finally, [the canonical plot with a thousandfold gap is obtained on Adam without weight decay and with a budget stretched to $10^{6}$ steps — deliberately, "to emphasise how late… generalisation may begin", whereas the default setting is AdamW with a weight decay of 1](#sec-a1-2). As an unconditional thesis the delay is refuted: by [the caption to figure 2 of Omnigrok](../2210.01117.omnigrok-grokking-beyond-algorithmic-data/original/2210.01117.omnigrok-grokking-beyond-algorithmic-data.md#fig-2), under a strong regularisation the generalisation is fast and the number of steps to it is inversely proportional to the weight decay — at a weight decay of order 1 there is no long delay. A model of accurate formulation appears in Grokfast itself, in its survey: `Power et al. [2022] demonstrated that various factors related to optimization … influence the model’s grokking pattern`. Details: [Grokfast (2405.20233)](../2405.20233.grokfast-accelerated-grokking-by-amplifying-slow-gradients/2405.20233.grokfast-accelerated-grokking-by-amplifying-slow-gradients.card.md#mc-2201-02177), [Xu, Vardi, Safran (2601.19791)](../2601.19791.to-grok-grokking-provable-grokking-in-ridge-regression/2601.19791.to-grok-grokking-provable-grokking-in-ridge-regression.card.md#mc-2201-02177), [Saheb Pasand, Dohmatob (2510.04930)](../2510.04930.egalitarian-gradient-descent-a-simple-approach-to-accelerated-grokking/2510.04930.egalitarian-gradient-descent-a-simple-approach-to-accelerated-grokking.card.md#mc-2201-02177). Milder definitional echoes of the same pattern: [2310.05918](../2310.05918.grokking-as-compression-a-nonlinear-complexity-perspective/2310.05918.grokking-as-compression-a-nonlinear-complexity-perspective.card.md), [2405.19454](../2405.19454.deep-grokking-would-deep-neural-networks-generalize-better/2405.19454.deep-grokking-would-deep-neural-networks-generalize-better.card.md), [2504.17243](../2504.17243.neuralgrok-accelerate-grokking-by-neural-gradient-transformation/2504.17243.neuralgrok-accelerate-grokking-by-neural-gradient-transformation.card.md), [2509.17738](../2509.17738.flatness-is-necessary-neural-collapse-is-not-rethinking-generalization-via-grokking/2509.17738.flatness-is-necessary-neural-collapse-is-not-rethinking-generalization-via-grokking.card.md), [2511.04760](../2511.04760.when-data-falls-short-grokking-below-the-critical-threshold/2511.04760.when-data-falls-short-grokking-below-the-critical-threshold.card.md), [2512.03437](../2512.03437.grokked-models-are-better-unlearners/2512.03437.grokked-models-are-better-unlearners.card.md), [2605.06152](../2605.06152.grokking-or-glitching-how-low-precision-drives-slingshot-loss-spikes/2605.06152.grokking-or-glitching-how-low-precision-drives-slingshot-loss-spikes.card.md), [2606.17120](../2606.17120.noise-driven-escape-from-metastable-phases-explains-grokking/2606.17120.noise-driven-escape-from-metastable-phases-explains-grokking.card.md).

###### p1-4

In this paper we propose to study generalization of neural networks on small algorithmically generated datasets. In this setting, questions about data efficiency, memorization, generalization, and speed of learning can be studied in great detail. In some situations we show that neural networks learn through a process of “grokking” a pattern in the data, improving generalization performance from random chance level to perfect generalization, and that this improvement in generalization can happen well past the point of overfitting. We also study generalization as a function of dataset size and find that smaller datasets require increasing amounts of optimization for generalization. We argue that these datasets provide a fertile ground for studying a poorly understood aspect of deep learning: generalization of overparametrized neural networks beyond memorization of the finite training dataset.

###### p2-1

The datasets we consider are binary operation tables of the form $a\circ b=c$ where $a,b,c$ are discrete symbols with no internal structure, and $\circ$ is a binary operation. Examples of binary operations include addition, composition of permutations, and bivariate polynomials. Training a neural network on a proper subset of all possible equations then amounts to filling in the blanks of the binary op table, much like solving a Sudoku puzzle. An example is shown on the right in Figure 1. Since we use distinct abstract symbols for all distinct elements $a,b,c$ involved in the equations, the network is not made aware of any internal structure of the elements, and has to learn about their properties only from their interactions with other elements. For example the network doesn’t see numbers in decimal notation, or permutations in line notation.

Our contributions are as follows:

###### p2-3

- We show that neural networks are capable of generalizing to the empty slots in a variety of binary op tables.
- We show that, long after severely overfitting, validation accuracy sometimes suddenly begins to increase from chance level toward perfect generalization. We call this phenomenon ‘grokking’. An example is shown in Figure 1.
- We present the data efficiency curves for a variety of binary operations.
- We show empirically that the amount of optimization required for generalization quickly increases as the dataset size decreases.
- We compare various optimization details to measure their impact on data efficiency. We find that weight decay is particularly effective at improving generalization on the tasks we study.
- We visualize the symbol embeddings learned by these networks and find that they sometimes uncover recognizable structure of the mathematical objects represented by the symbols.

### 3.2 Grokking on a variety of problems

###### p3-4

Some of the operations listed in Figure 2 (right) are symmetric with respect to the order of the operands ($x+y$, $x*y$, $x^{2}+y^{2}$ and $x^{2}+xy+y^{2}$). Such operations tend to require less data for generalization than closely related non-symmetrical counterparts ($x-y$, $x/y$, $x^{2}+xy+y^{2}+x$). We believe this effect might be partially architecture-dependent, since it’s easy for a transformer to learn a symmetric function of the operands by ignoring positional embedding.

Some operations (for example $x^{3}+xy^{2}+y\pmod{97}$) didn’t lead to generalization within the allowed optimization budget at any percentage of data up to 95%. The converged models effectively just memorized the training dataset without finding any real patterns in the data. To such a model, the data is effectively random.

The operation $[x/y\pmod{p}\text{ if }y\text{ is odd, otherwise }x-y\pmod{p}]$ requires the network to learn a mix of several simple operations - in particular the role of $x$ has to be interpreted as a residue in the additive group when it’s paired with an even $y$, and as a residue in the multiplicative group when it’s paired with an odd $y$. This shows that generalization can happen even for operations that are not cleanly interpretable via group or ring operations.

###### sec-3-3

### 3.3 Ablations and Tricks

###### p4-1

We find that adding weight decay has a very large effect on data efficiency, more than halving the amount of samples needed compared to most other interventions. We found that weight decay towards the initialization of the network is also effective, but not quite as effective as weight decay towards the origin. This makes us believe that the prior, that approximately zero weights are suitable for small algorithmic tasks, explains part, but not all of the superior performance of weight decay. Adding some noise to the optimization process (e.g. gradient noise from using minibatches, Gaussian noise applied to weights before or after computing the gradients) is beneficial for generalization, consistent with the idea that such noise might induce the optimization to find flatter minima that generalize better. We found that learning rate had to be tuned in a relatively narrow window for the generalization to happen (within 1 order of magnitude).

### A.3 Related Work

###### p7-6

In this paper we study training and generalization dynamics on small simple algorithmic datasets. In the past, algorithmic datasets have been used to probe the capability of neural networks to perform symbolic and algorithmic reasoning. For example the tasks of copying, reversing, and sorting randomly generated sequences, and performing arithmetic operations of multi-digit numbers, have been used as standard benchmarks for sequence-to-sequence models Graves et al. (2014), Weston et al. (2014) Kaiser & Sutskever (2015) Reed & De Freitas (2015), Grefenstette et al. (2015), Zaremba & Sutskever (2015), Graves (2016), Dehghani et al. (2018). Typically in these works however the emphasis is on the performance in the unlimited data regime, with generalization often studied with respect to input sequence length. Some papers study the sample complexity on algorithmic tasks Reed & De Freitas (2015), but mostly focus on the impact of architectural choices. In contrast we study the phenomenon of generalization in data-limited regime, with an emphasis on phenomena that we believe to be architecture-agnostic.

**[p. 8]**

Algorithmically generated reasoning datasets like bAbI Weston et al. (2015) encourage work on studying generalization in data-limited regime. Most results on such datasets however focus on a point estimate of performance of a particular architecture or training technique, whereas our main interest is in pointing out the change in generalization past the point where a particular architecture can memorize the training data completely.

Neelakantan et al. (2015) has a “grok-like” learning curve on an algorithmic task, but it is related to optimization difficulty, whereas our phenomenon is specifically about generalization.

In Saxton et al. (2019) they study generalization on procedurally generated math problems such as arithmetic and differentiation, but for the most part these tasks are more involved than the simple binary op problems we have studied and as such do not lend themselves to observing the kinds of phenomena we describe in this paper, since they would require an extremely large number of samples to master.

###### p8-4

In Jiang et al. (2019) they studied a large number of generalization or complexity measures on convolutional neural networks to see which, if any, are predictive of generalization performance. They find that flatness based measures that aim to quantify the sensitivity of the trained neural network to parameter perturbations are the most predictive. We conjectured that the grokking phenomena **[p. 9]** we report in this work may be due to the noise from SGD driving the optimization to flatter/simpler solutions that generalize better and hope to investigate in future work whether any of these measures are predictive of grokking.

Zhang et al. (2016) finds that neural networks of sizes typically used in deep learning can interpolate arbitrary training data, and yet generalize when trained with semantically meaningful labels using appropriate optimization procedures. Our work shows a related phenomenon where neural networks can interpolate a small algorithmic training dataset without generalizing, but start generalizing when trained with SGD for longer.

###### p9-2

Nakkiran et al. (2019); Belkin et al. (2018) focus on the phenomenon of double descent in loss as a function of model and optimization procedure capacity. They find that the classical U-shaped validation loss curve is followed in some settings (including neural network training) by a second descent of loss that starts around the minimal capacity that is needed to interpolate any training data. We observe a second descent in validation loss (though not accuracy) as a function of the amount of training in some of our experiments, and it happens past the point of interpolating the training data. We believe that the phenomenon we describe might be distinct from the double descent phenomena described in Nakkiran et al. (2019); Belkin et al. (2018) because we observe the second descent in loss far past the first time the training loss becomes very small (tens of thousands of epochs in some of our experiments), and we don’t observe a non-monotonic behavior of accuracy. The setting of small algorithmic datasets that we study also provides a smaller, more tractable playground for studying subtle generalization phenomena than natural datasets studied in Nakkiran et al. (2019).

*Figure 6: Effect on data efficiency of introducing $k\in[0,10,100,1000,2000,3000]$ outliers (examples with random labels) into the training data. Small number of outliers doesn’t noticeably impact generalization performance, but a large number hinders it significantly.* **[p. 9]**

In this section we show data efficiency curves for a modified version of a binary op by introducing $k$ outliers to the training dataset. More precisely, at the beginning of the experiment we randomly sample $k$ equations from the training set and replace their answers with answers to other $k$ equations randomly sampled from the training data. The rest of the equations in the training data and all the equations in the validation data are kept as before.

In this situation one could imagine one of the following scenarios unfolding. If the model class of neural networks optimized and regularized as before was not large enough to interpolate such “noisy” dataset, one could imagine the procedure converging to a solution that generalizes well, but denoises the training data (i.e. predicts $c=a\circ b$ as an answer even for the outlier equations $a,b\to c^{\prime}$ with $c^{\prime}\neq c$). On the other extreme it could be that the optimization procedure can find networks that interpolate the data, but the resulting models don’t generalize, because they are forced to represent a considerably more complicated function than before (a simple function + $k$ exceptions encoded in the training data).

###### p9-5

In our experiments we find that the first option doesn’t happen - all experiments reach 100% training accuracy at some point, and this point is not considerably affected by changing the number of outliers $k$. The second phenomenon happens in a range of training data percentages and number of outliers $k$ - increasing $k$ decreases the range of training data percentages for which the optimization procedure converges to models that generalize. However the effect of introducing a small number of outliers (up to 1000) is not very pronounced - see Figure 6. We interpret this as additional evidence that the capacity of the network and optimization procedure is well beyond the capacity needed for **[p. 10]** memorizing all the labels on the training data, and that generalization happening at all requires a non-trivial explanation.
