## Concepts covered

**[Grokking](../../concepts/grokking.md)** — the work takes the phenomenon as given and asks not "why does it exist" but "what sets its speed": the definition is taken word for word from Power et al. — the network reaches a nearly zero training loss but generalises only after a long delay ([p. 1, para. 5](#p1-5)) — and there is no dispute about the definition itself in the paper. Its own move is *modulators*: architectural and optimisational levers that systematically change grokking behaviour ([p. 1, para. 8](#p1-8)); the order in which generalisation sets in under different levers is what is measured. What the work does not contain: no explanation of why the delay arises at all, and no threshold or measure of the delay beyond "a test loss below $0.01$" ([fig. 2](#fig-2)); every conclusion is taken on one task at one modulus and one split, and the narrowness of the scope is declared a merit rather than a caveat ([p. 1, para. 8](#p1-8)).

**[The lazy→rich transition](../../concepts/lazy-to-rich-kernel-to-feature-learning.md)** — here lies the work's chief, and a negative, contribution. The dispute is not about the phenomenon but about the **legitimacy of a lever**: the correction of the learning rate $\eta=\eta_{0}/\alpha^{2}$, by which Kumar et al. keep the update norms comparable across output scales, is derived for a mean-squared loss and SGD and loses its sense in this setting ([p. 5, para. 8](#p5-8)). Under AdamW with $\epsilon\to0$ the factor $\alpha$ cancels of itself ([p. 6, para. 2](#p6-2)), so dividing the step by $\alpha^{2}$ changes the effective step and the strength of the regularisation rather than the "laziness"; and at a fixed $\eta_{0}$ the sign of the relation reverses, and $\alpha$ behaves as an inverse softmax temperature ([p. 7, para. 1](#p7-1)). Whence the conclusion of the section and of the paper: the "lazy to rich" reading is not borne out here ([p. 7, para. 2](#p7-2), [p. 8, para. 2](#p8-2)). What the work does not contain: the drift from the NTK at initialisation — what defines the regime in Kumar et al. — is never measured here, and the claim that "the network operates predominantly in the rich regime" ([p. 2, para. 1](#p2-1)) rests only on the effective rank and the spectrum changing ([p. 7, para. 8](#p7-8)); nor is there a direct reproduction of the setting of Kumar et al. (SGD, a squared loss) in the same setting.

**[The neural tangent kernel](../../concepts/neural-tangent-kernel-ntk.md)** — the notion is brought in only in the survey of related work and only as a language for describing the lazy regime: in the lazy regime the network "departs negligibly from its NTK at initialisation", that is, it stays in the linearised form the NTK admits, whereas in the rich regime the NTK is still definable but the departure from it is substantial ([Appendix A](#p12-1)). What matters for the corpus is the asymmetry: it is precisely the drift from the NTK that serves as the definition of the regime in Kumar et al., and it is precisely that which is not computed in this work — the conclusion about the rich regime is drawn from the variability of the effective rank and the feature spectrum ([p. 7, para. 8](#p7-8)). What the work does not contain: not a single computation of a kernel and not a single reference to the NTK beyond the survey of related work.

**[The optimiser](../../concepts/optimizer-adam-adamw-sgd.md)** — the work gives the corpus a rarity: an exact and checkable argument that the choice of optimiser changes the sense of someone else's lever. The derivation ([p. 6, para. 1](#p6-1)) shows that under AdamW with $\epsilon\to0$ the update $\Delta\theta\approx-\eta\,m_{f}/\sqrt{v_{f}}$ no longer depends on $\alpha$, whence the authors' formulation: "using AdamW instead of SGD changes how the output scale affects the network" ([p. 6, para. 2](#p6-2)). A second, implementational argument: in PyTorch and JAX the weight decay in AdamW is multiplied by the learning rate, so the $1/\alpha^{2}$ correction confounds the strength of the regularisation as well ([p. 6, para. 2](#p6-2)). What the work does not contain: a comparison of optimisers as an experiment — SGD is never run, and the argument remains a derivation plus a check of the update norms under AdamW.

**[The learning rate](../../concepts/learning-rate.md)** — the lever is re-checked and serves as the support of the chief argument. The direct experiment: the higher the learning rate at a fixed weight decay, the faster the generalisation ([fig. 4](#fig-4), panel a); and the update norms in the early epochs are not comparable ([p. 6, para. 4](#p6-4)). Then the learning rate is fixed at $\eta_{0}=0.001$ for all $\alpha$, and it is precisely this fixing that reverses the sign of the relation between the output scale and the speed of grokking ([p. 7, para. 1](#p7-1)). What the work does not contain: the swept grid is nowhere named in the text — it can be read only from the legends of [fig. 4](#fig-4) ($\eta\in\{0.001,0.003,0.005,0.01\}$) and [fig. 5](#fig-5) ($\alpha\in\{0.5,0.75,1.0,1.25,1.5\}$); and the dependence of the grokking time on the learning rate is reduced to no number and is legible only from the curves.

**[Weight decay](../../concepts/weight-decay.md)** — the second optimisational lever, and the one that stitches two sections of the work together. The direct part: the higher the weight decay at a fixed learning rate, the faster the generalisation ([fig. 4](#fig-4), panel c). The indirect and more valuable part: a larger weight decay gives a greater compressibility of the features — a lower effective rank, a lower power-law exponent, a heavier spectral tail ([p. 7, para. 10](#p7-10), [fig. 9](#fig-9)) — that is, the same measure that orders the LN placements orders the weight-decay strengths too. An implementational remark matters separately for the corpus: in PyTorch and JAX the strength of the weight decay in AdamW is multiplied by the learning rate ([p. 6, para. 2](#p6-2)), so "the same $\lambda$" at different steps does not mean the same regularisation. What the work does not contain: the swept values of $\lambda$ in the text — they are legible only from the legends of [fig. 4](#fig-4) and [fig. 9](#fig-9) ($\lambda\in\{0.1,0.2,0.5,0.75,1.0,1.5\}$), whereas the setting names a single base $\lambda=1.0$ ([Appendix C](#p13-1)); and the question of whether weight decay is necessary for grokking is not posed at all.

**[The canonical grokking proving ground](../../concepts/canonical-grokking-testbed.md)** — the setting is taken from Nanda et al. unchanged and declared as such: "We follow (35) and use a single-layer transformer of width 128" — 4 heads, an MLP hidden layer of 512, no weight tying in the unembedding, full batch, 10000 epochs, AdamW, $\eta=0.001$, $\lambda=1.0$, and the HookedTransformer initialisation ([Appendix C](#p13-1)). The work's contribution to the notion is not a new setting but a demonstration that one node of this setting (the placement of LN), not usually discussed, changes the grokking time by an order of magnitude. What the work does not contain: no second split, no second modulus and no second operation; the robustness is checked only over seeds (5 per setting), over the normalisation (RMSNorm, [fig. 7](#fig-7)) and over the depth (3 layers at width 64, [fig. 8](#fig-8)).

**[Modular arithmetic](../../concepts/modular-arithmetic.md)** — the work's only task, and the choice is motivated precisely by its canonicity: "We use the setting of single-layer transformers learning modular addition: it is the most widely studied grokking setting in transformers" ([p. 2, para. 4](#p2-4)). The task is taken at $p=113$, three tokens $(a,b,=)$, with only the last unembedded ([p. 2, para. 4](#p2-4)). What the work does not contain: no modular multiplication, no other operations and no carry-over to non-algorithmic data; the authors themselves place this among the limitations ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4)).

**[Fourier features and circuits](../../concepts/fourier-features-circuits.md)** — the work reduces the picture of Nanda and Zhong to a single number: the Fourier-mode concentration $\mathcal{C}_{\text{Fourier}}$, the ratio of between-mode to within-mode variance in the Fourier transform of the embedding matrix, declared akin to an ANOVA F-statistic, with a threshold of $1.0$ ([p. 3, para. 5](#p3-5)). The substantive result for the notion: the placement of LN changes the **time** rather than the **kind** of solution — "all the variants arrive at periodically structured common solutions regardless of the LN placement" ([p. 3, para. 5](#p3-5)) — and the order in which $\mathcal{C}_{\text{Fourier}}$ passes $1.0$ coincides with the order of generalisation ([fig. 2](#fig-2)). What the work does not contain: an analysis of the circuits themselves — no ablations and no isolation of heads or neurons; $\mathcal{C}_{\text{Fourier}}$ is computed only over the embedding matrix rather than over the neuron-to-logit map, and no comparison with the measures of Nanda et al. is made.

**[Attention routing](../../concepts/attention-routing-heads.md)** — the work's most singular observation: one and the same operation acts in opposite ways on different attention paths. LN on the value channel alone ($\mathbf{A^{v}}$) accelerates the grokking greatly, whereas LN on the queries and keys alone ($\mathbf{A^{qk}}$) fails to generalise within 10 thousand epochs and lags behind even **No LN** ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3), [fig. 3](#fig-3)). The mechanism is named: removing the scale of the embeddings on the queries and keys lowers the entropy of the softmax distribution of the attention scores, whereas the trigonometric composition after Nanda/Zhong requires representations at both positions $a$ and $b$ ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3)). What the work does not contain: an analysis by head (there are four) and causal ablations — the entropy is measured averaged over rows, and the relation "low entropy → a limited composition" is set out as an argument rather than checked by intervention.

**[The memorisation phase](../../concepts/memorization-phase.md)** — the work gives a cheap measure of what the network relies on during the memorisation era. *SR-train* is the training loss under the substitution $\text{MLP}(\bullet)\to\text{MLP}(\bullet/\|\bullet\|)$, measured with no backpropagation ([p. 4, para. 2](#p4-2)). In **No LN** it is far higher than the ordinary training loss, that is, the network relies on the scale of the features rather than on their directions; and, above all, "SR-train decreases as the delayed descent of the test loss, that is, the grokking, occurs, and increases when the network memorises at a nearly zero training loss and a large test loss" ([p. 4, para. 2](#p4-2)). Memorisation is thereby identified as a shortcut through the scale of the MLP activations. What the work does not contain: SR-train is not decomposed by layer and is not compared with any other measure of memorisation; and the attribution "the MLP memorises" itself is inherited from another work (17) rather than checked here.

**[Compression of the representation manifold](../../concepts/manifold-representation-compression.md)** — the measure of compressibility is the effective rank $\operatorname{ER}(Z)$ of the correlation matrix of the pre-logits of the "=" token ([p. 7, para. 7](#p7-7)). The work's claim: the onset of compressibility is ordered in the same way as the grokking, and this holds for two different levers at once — the placement of LN and the strength of the weight decay: "settings that generalise earlier arrive at a greater compressibility earlier" ([p. 7, para. 10](#p7-10), [fig. 6](#fig-6), [fig. 9](#fig-9)). A caveat is made by the authors themselves: $\mathbf{A^{*}}$ does not attain a lower minimum effective rank than **No LN** but merely comes within $10\%$ of its own minimum slightly earlier ([p. 7, para. 9](#p7-9)). What the work does not contain: no causal check (the rank is nowhere imposed by force) and no numerical values — the whole relation is legible from the curves.

**[Spectral analysis / FVE](../../concepts/spectral-analysis-svd-esd-fve.md)** — the second measure of compressibility: "we fit a power law to the eigenvalue spectrum of the correlation matrix of the features" (the `powerlaw` package), and the exponent $\alpha$ is the lower the heavier the tail ([p. 7, para. 7](#p7-7); the measure's code is given in full in Appendix D). It matters that the spectrum is taken from the feature matrix — the pre-logits on the training set — rather than from the weight matrices. The work gives the corpus the link "a lower power-law exponent in the features means an earlier generalisation", traced simultaneously over the LN placement ([fig. 6](#fig-6)) and over the weight-decay strength ([fig. 9](#fig-9)). What the work does not contain: no fitting range, no confidence bounds and no check of the fit's quality; noise spikes are not analysed but suppressed by aggregating with a minimum over 5 seeds ([fig. 6](#fig-6)).

**[Heavy-tailed self-regularisation](../../concepts/heavy-tailed-self-regularization-htsr.md)** — the work arrives at heavy spectral tails from another side and in another language. The quantity is the same in form — the exponent of a power-law fit of an eigenspectrum — but it is fitted to the correlation matrix of the **features** rather than to the layers' weight matrices, and the justification is taken from Agrawal et al. ($\alpha$-Req) rather than from HTSR: "higher weight-decay values show heavier-tailed eigenspectra in $Z$" ([p. 7, para. 10](#p7-10)). What the work does not contain: not a word of HTSR, no WeightWatcher and no reference to that line of work; thresholds of the kind "$\alpha\approx2$" do not appear here, and conclusions about the spectrum of the weights cannot be credited to the work.

**[Progress measures](../../concepts/progress-measures.md)** — the work claims its compressibility measure as a candidate progress measure, and does so twice with different force. In the conclusion, with no caveat: existing studies of generalising features "yield a setting-independent progress measure for grokking" ([p. 8, para. 3](#p8-3)); in the limitations section, with a hedge: "reveals a plausibly setting-independent progress measure for grokking" ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4)). The support is that $\operatorname{ER}(Z)$ and the exponent $\alpha$ order the settings identically under two dissimilar levers. What the work does not contain: a check of the "setting-independence" in even one second setting — the measure is chosen and tested in one and the same; and unlike the measures of Nanda et al. it does not anticipate the jump but moves alongside it, that is, its predictive ability was not measured.

**[The slingshot effect](../../concepts/slingshot.md)** — slingshots are observed three times in the work and are never analysed. In [fig. 2](#fig-2) the settings *MLP Pre-LN* and *Pre* "show slingshots", and their curves are drawn at a lower opacity; in [fig. 6](#fig-6) the power-law exponent is obtained by aggregating with a minimum over 5 seeds precisely "to eliminate the noise spikes caused by slingshots"; and at depth 3 "a greater depth shows substantially more slingshots (45)" ([fig. 8](#fig-8)). The reference to Thilak et al. appears once and serves as a name for the phenomenon. What the work does not contain: no measurement of the loss spikes, no cycles of the last layer's weight norm and no relation of the slingshots to grokking — here they are a nuisance to the measurement rather than a subject.

**[The role of gradient noise](../../concepts/gradient-noise.md)** — the whole work is done without batch noise: "The optimisation is always performed full-batch over $10000$ epochs by means of AdamW" ([Appendix C](#p13-1)). For the corpus this places the work in the chain of full-batch studies of grokking and means that all the spread reported is over initialisations (5 seeds) rather than over data order. What the work does not contain: a comparison with batch training and any discussion of the batch size whatever — it is not even named as a variable.

**[The data fraction / the critical dataset size](../../concepts/data-fraction-critical-dataset-size.md)** — the fraction is fixed and not swept: "The split of the data into training and test is $30$-$70\%$ at a total of $p^{2}$ samples" ([Appendix C](#p13-1)). This matters in that the training fraction here is lower than in Nanda et al., and so all the work's grokking times are not directly comparable with their numbers. What the work does not contain: not a single point at another fraction and no mention of a critical dataset size; data starvation is named once, and that in a retelling of others' works ([Appendix A](#p12-3)).

**[The Clock and Pizza algorithms](../../concepts/clock-vs-pizza.md)** — the notion is only mentioned, but the mention is substantive for two things. First, Zhong et al. are brought in as an argument about the non-uniqueness of the solution: they "find that this solution is not unique and show that transformers can learn a „pizza“ algorithm" in addition to the "clock" ([Appendix A](#p12-3)). Second, the mechanistic picture on which the analysis of attention entropy leans is taken from Nanda and Zhong at once as a single one ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3)) — the difference between the "clock" and the "pizza" is not used in the analysis. What the work does not contain: not a single measurement distinguishing the algorithms — $\mathcal{C}_{\text{Fourier}}$ is insensitive to such a distinction; and it is precisely to this place that the erroneous reference analysed below pertains.

---

**[Architectural inductive bias](../../concepts/architectural-inductive-bias.md)** — the claim is formulated outright: biases expressed through the architecture modulate grokking ([`p2-1`](#p2-1)). The probe is the placement of layer normalisation — the same layers in a different order give strikingly different times at identically structured solutions.
## Points we could not reconcile

**The setting refers to the wrong section.** [Appendix C](#p13-1) says that "the settings given in Section 2.3 are used to create the transformer variants", whereas the LN settings are defined in Section 2.2 (Table 1); Section 2.3 is already the experiments with them.

**The analysis of the learning rate and the weight decay refers to neighbouring panels.** §3.2 says: "As can be seen in Figure 4a and Figure 4b respectively, higher learning rates and weight-decay strengths lead to a faster generalisation and less grokking" ([p. 6, para. 4](#p6-4)). By the caption of [fig. 4](#fig-4) the weight decay is depicted in panel c, while panel b shows the parameter-update norms at different learning rates. Two sentences later the same paragraph refers to 4b correctly, as the update norms.

**The attention scores are obtained by projecting the wrong matrices.** In ② it is said that the embeddings at the query and key inputs "decisively compose the attention scores after the projections $W^{Q}$ and $W^{V}$" ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3)); by Table 2 of Appendix B and by the sense of the paragraph it ought to be $W^{K}$ — further in the same paragraph the subject is "the product of queries and keys", and the value channel is analysed separately in ③.

**Two settings from a figure caption are nowhere defined.** The caption of [fig. 2](#fig-2) names the configurations *MLP Pre-LN* and *Pre* as showing slingshots; no such names appear either in Table 1 or in the text, and they are not reconciled with the four defined settings (**No LN**, $\mathbf{A^{*}}$, $\mathbf{M}$+$\mathbf{A^{*}}$, $\mathbf{M}$). The reverse of the same: $\mathbf{A^{qk}}$ and $\mathbf{A^{v}}$, on which the whole analysis of §2.4 rests, are not entered in Table 1 and live only in the text ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3), [p. 5, para. 1](#p5-1)).

**"The features of all the data points" against "the training set only".** The setting of §4.1 says outright: "Importantly, we use only data points from the training set and not from the test set, in order to check whether the training data is fitted lazily" ([p. 7, para. 7](#p7-7)). The conclusion, however, formulates the result more widely: "we show how the features of all the data points change substantially and continuously over the course of the training process" ([p. 8, para. 3](#p8-3)). The difference matters: the claim about the rich regime is made from the features of the training points.

**The force of the central claim changes from section to section.** One and the same conclusion about the training regime is given three times with different force: in the list of contributions, categorically — "These observations show that the network operates predominantly in the rich regime" ([p. 2, para. 1](#p2-1)); in the abstract, with a hedge — "which suggests that grokking in transformers may be subtler than a lazy-to-rich transition of the training regime" ([p. 1, para. 2](#p1-2)); in the conclusion, again with a hedge — "Our findings suggest that the network learns features richly throughout" ([p. 8, para. 3](#p8-3)). The same with the progress measure: in the conclusion it is "setting-independent" ([p. 8, para. 3](#p8-3)), in the limitations "plausibly setting-independent" ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4)). When citing one should take the hedged formulation: it agrees with what is measured.

## What is measured, what is shown and what is conjectured

**Derived before the experiment and confirmed by it.** The analysis of the correction $\eta=\eta_{0}/\alpha^{2}$ is the one part of the work in which a prediction is made by derivation and then checked. From the form of the cross-entropy derivative it follows that $g_{h}$ grows with $\alpha$ approximately linearly rather than quadratically ([p. 5, para. 8](#p5-8)); from the form of the AdamW update at $\epsilon\to0$, that the factor $\alpha$ cancels and "the parameter updates would be approximately comparable across $\alpha$, at least in the early stages of the training" ([p. 6, para. 2](#p6-2)). Both consequences are checked: at $\eta=\eta_{0}/\alpha^{2}$ the update norms diverge, at a fixed $\eta_{0}$ they are comparable, and the sign of the relation between $\alpha$ and the speed of grokking reverses ([fig. 5](#fig-5), [p. 7, para. 1](#p7-1)). This is the work's soundest part.

**Shown by experiment, but in one setting.** The order of the LN settings by speed of grokking ([p. 3, para. 4](#p3-4)), the coincidence of that order with the order in which $\mathcal{C}_{\text{Fourier}}$ passes $1.0$ ([p. 3, para. 5](#p3-5)) and with the order in which compressibility sets in ([p. 7, para. 9](#p7-9)), the behaviour of SR-train ([p. 4, para. 2](#p4-2)), and the failure of $\mathbf{A^{qk}}$ and the acceleration under $\mathbf{A^{v}}$ ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3), [p. 5, para. 1](#p5-1)) — all of this is taken at 5 seeds at $p=113$, one split, one optimiser and one task. The robustness is checked over the normalisation (RMSNorm, [fig. 7](#fig-7)) and over the depth (3 layers, [fig. 8](#fig-8)), but not over the task, the modulus or the data fraction.

**Declared but not measured.** The claim that "the network operates predominantly in the rich regime" ([p. 2, para. 1](#p2-1)) rests on one paragraph with not a single number and no reference to a figure ([p. 7, para. 8](#p7-8)): all that is shown is that $\operatorname{ER}(Z)$ and the spectrum change continuously. A continuous change of features is not a definition of the rich regime — in Kumar et al. the regime is defined by the drift from the NTK at initialisation, and that quantity is not computed here. Likewise the conclusion that the "lazy to rich" reading is inapplicable ([p. 7, para. 2](#p7-2)) remains explained but not checked directly: there is no experiment in the work reproducing the setting of Kumar et al. (SGD, a squared loss) on the same task.

**The numbers live in the figures rather than in the text.** Not one summary number — a grokking time, a ratio of times, a value of a measure — appears in the text of the paper; everything is read off the curves and points. The right-hand panel of [fig. 1](#fig-1) is the only place with a scale of "epochs to a loss $\leq1\%$" separately for the training and test sets, and it is precisely that which supports §2.3's claim about the decoupling of the fitting times ([p. 3, para. 4](#p3-4)); the figure's caption, meanwhile, describes only its left, schematic part and says not a word about the bar chart. The grids of swept values ($\eta$, $\lambda$, $\alpha$) are named only in the legends of [fig. 4](#fig-4) and [fig. 5](#fig-5). The claimed "quite symmetric" relation between the time at which $\mathcal{C}_{\text{Fourier}}$ passes $1.0$ and the time of generalisation ([p. 3, para. 5](#p3-5)) rests on a scatter plot of four points — one per LN setting ([fig. 2](#fig-2), panel c); neither a coefficient of association nor a definition of "symmetry" is given.

**Limits acknowledged by the authors.** The work "is not an exhaustive study of all possible LN placements", and the findings "may not carry over to other settings" ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4)). It is worth keeping in mind separately that LN is chosen as a lever on the pretext that "it changes the training but not the capacity of the model" ([p. 2, para. 4](#p2-4)), whereas the learnable $\gamma,\beta$ do change the capacity — if only by $2d$ parameters.

## Common misreadings and overstatements

Editorial notes on the places where this work conveys others' results with an error or without the authors' caveats. The "details" links from the "Erroneous retellings and citations of this paper" sections in the cards of the cited works lead here.

###### mc-2306-17844

**Zhong et al. (2306.17844) — the phase transitions between "clock" and "pizza" credited to a different work.** The survey of related work says: `"Moreover, transformers can exhibit sharp phase transitions (4) between these two solutions depending on width and attention strength."` ([Appendix A](#p12-3)). The reference (4) is Akyürek et al., "What learning algorithm is in-context learning?", a work about in-context learning by linear models, in which there is no "clock", no "pizza" and no dependence on width and attention strength. The result belongs to [Zhong et al. 2023](../2306.17844.the-clock-and-the-pizza-two-stories-in-mechanistic-explanation-of-neural-networks/2306.17844.the-clock-and-the-pizza-two-stories-in-mechanistic-explanation-of-neural-networks.card.md), who themselves introduce both sides of the comparison.

The origin of the error is traceable exactly. In Zhong et al. the sentence in the introduction is built thus: `"Models exhibit sharp *algorithmic phase transitions* [6] between the *Clock* and *Pizza* algorithms as their width and attention strength very"`, and the reference [6] there is a reference to the **term** "algorithmic phase transitions" rather than to a result; [6] in their bibliography is indeed Akyürek et al. In retelling it, Singh et al. preserved both the sentence and the position of the reference but removed the italics and the word "algorithmic", whereupon the reference ceased to read as a reference to a term and came to read as the source of the finding.

It matters that in the neighbouring sentences of the same paragraph that same work is cited accurately: the non-uniqueness of the solution and the "pizza" algorithm are assigned to (54), that is, to Zhong et al., and the "clock" algorithm to (35), Nanda et al. ([Appendix A](#p12-3)). The warning here concerns the formulation rather than the work.

## Common misreadings and overstatements of this paper

Patterns observed in the citing works of the corpus. The work appeared in February 2026 and has so far two citing works in the corpus; both convey its chief thesis close to the abstract, and neither distorts it outright. Below is what is lost in the process.

**"LayerNorm accelerates grokking" (accurate, but requiring a caveat).** Both formulations observed take the conclusion in one direction: `"Singh et al. 2026 demonstrate that architectural choices such as layer-normalization placement strongly modulate grokking dynamics"` (2605.20441, no card) and `"In concurrent work, Singh et al. (2026) found that restricting LayerNorm placement can accelerate generalization by limiting reliance on activation scale"` ([Yildirim 2026](../2603.05228.the-geometric-inductive-bias-of-grokking-bypassing-phase-transitions-via-architectural-topology/2603.05228.the-geometric-inductive-bias-of-grokking-bypassing-phase-transitions-via-architectural-topology.card.md)). Both are correct as to the abstract ([p. 1, para. 2](#p1-2)) and as to the mechanism the authors do establish through SR-train ([p. 4, para. 2](#p4-2)). What is lost is the asymmetry that makes the result substantive: the **same** operation on a different path does not accelerate the grokking but destroys it — the setting $\mathbf{A^{qk}}$ does not generalise within 10 thousand epochs and lags behind a network with no LN at all ([p. 4, para. 3](#p4-3), [fig. 3](#fig-3)). The formula "LN helps" does not follow from the work; what follows is "the placement of LN decides whether it helps or hinders".

**"It is refuted that grokking is a lazy-to-rich transition" (an expected widening).** No such formulation has yet been observed in the corpus, but the work's headline conclusion invites it: "our work argues against the „lazy to rich“ view (28) in transformers learning modular addition" ([p. 8, para. 2](#p8-2)). What is shown is narrower: **one particular lever** of laziness — the output scale with the step correction $\eta=\eta_{0}/\alpha^{2}$ — is derived for a mean-squared loss and SGD and does not carry over to AdamW with cross-entropy ([p. 5, para. 8](#p5-8), [p. 6, para. 2](#p6-2)). The transition between the regimes itself was never measured here: there is no drift from the NTK at initialisation in the work, and the conclusion about the rich regime is drawn from the effective rank and the spectrum changing continuously ([p. 7, para. 8](#p7-8)). The authors' own formulation in §3 is more careful than the headline one and will serve as a model: "the characterisation of the training regime here becomes subtler than a lazy-to-rich transition" ([p. 7, para. 2](#p7-2)).

We investigate grokking in transformers through the lens of inductive bias: dispositions arising from architecture or optimization that let the network prefer one solution over another. Given a neural network $f(\bullet\;\ \theta)$ with parameters $\theta$, (28) scale the network’s output $f(x\;\theta)$ by a fixed scalar $\alpha>0$ to get $h(x\;\theta)=\alpha f(x\;\theta)$ to control laziness, inspired from (13). Then, the gradient $g_{h}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta$ of a loss function $\mathcal{L}$ for network $h$ would be

$$
g_{h} =\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial h(x\ ;\theta)}\ \frac{\partial h(x\ ;\theta)}{\partial\theta} \tag{7}
$$

$$
=\alpha\cdot\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial h(x\ ;\theta)}\ \frac{\partial f(x\ ;\theta)}{\partial\theta} \tag{8}
$$

If $\mathcal{L}$ is the mean-squared error (MSE loss), i.e., for some input $x_{i}$ given to $h$ and ground truth $y_{i}$, $\mathcal{L}$ is given by

$$
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(h(x_{i}\ ;\theta)-y_{i})^{2}. \tag{9}
$$

Consequently, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial h(x\;\theta)=\sum_{i}(\alpha f(x_{i}\;\theta)-y_{i})$ which scales linearly with $\alpha$ and so

$$
g_{h}=\alpha\left(\sum_{i}(\alpha f(x_{i}\ ;\theta)-y_{i})\right)(\partial f(x_{i}\ ;\theta)/\partial\theta)\sim\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}). \tag{10}
$$

###### p5-8

Thus the gradient and $\|\Delta\theta\|=\|-\eta\ g_{h}\|$ scales as $\alpha^{2}$ (ignoring weight decay). Then to keep weight update norms comparable in order to only change laziness and not effective step size, i.e., to ensure $\|\Delta\theta\|\approx\eta_{0}\|g_{f}\|$ is fixed across $\alpha$, the learning rate must change as $\eta=\eta_{0}/\alpha^{2}$. This learning rate scaling is followed in (28) to present the lazy-to-rich interpretation of grokking. However, *scaling the learning rate so is only applicable for MSE loss and SGD*. If we now consider $\mathcal{L}$ to be the cross-entropy loss

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial h(x\ ;\theta)_{j}}=(p(\alpha f(x\ ;\theta))_{j}-y_{j}) \tag{11}
$$

where $p(\bullet)$ is the softmax operation. Hence for cross-entropy loss, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial h(x\;\theta)$ does not scale linearly with $\alpha$. Hence, $g_{h}$ scales approximately linearly (certainly sub-quadratically) due to one factor of $\alpha$ coming from $\partial h(x\;\theta)/\partial\theta$. ###### p12-1

Feature learning, or the ability of a model to change its activations in order to solve a given task has been a core area of interest in machine learning research. Works like (13) show that it is possible to perform well on tasks without changing the network’s parameters and features, i.e, learning can occur in the lazy regime. Moreover, initial weight norm, readout scale, and learning rate were found to be among the factors that can govern whether a network operates in the lazy or rich regime of feature learning. The seminal work of Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) (23) has been integral in characterizing the behavior of neural networks, and becomes relevant to feature learning as in the lazy regime, the network drifts negligibly from its NTK at initialization. In other words, the network stays in its linearized form as admitted by the NTK at initialization. Notably, the NTK can still be defined even when the network operates in the rich regime, however, drift from NTK at initialization would be significant in this case. Consequently, this line of research has motivates other studies which investigate distinctions and overlaps between the conditions inducing these regimes. For instance, (50; 42; 25; 15) have shown that conditions inducing lazy learning can also admit rich feature learning, and that the rich regime can allow networks to perform better than when they learn lazily.

###### p12-3

Grokking has emerged as a bridge between mechanistic interpretability (37; 38; 48; 51) and the study of training dynamics (29; 30; 16). The setting we study, i.e., the popular modular addition task (21) ($a+b\mod p$) learned by transformers was first mechanistically analyzed by (35) to reveal that generalizing solutions contained periodic structure in the parameter matrices of the transformer, such as the embedding matrix and the neuron-logit map. Input tokens that require addition, i.e, $a$ and $b$ are mapped to rotations $(\sin(\omega a),\cos(\omega a))$ and $(\sin(\omega b),\cos(\omega b))$ respectively by the embedding matrix. These rotations are then composed by the transformer using trigonometric identities to then depict $(\sin(\omega(a+b)),\cos(\omega(a+b))$ in the feature of the last token. This feature is then unembedded to predict the current result of the modulus. Interestingly, (54) find that this solution is not unique, and show that transformers can learn the “pizza” algorithm in addition to several other solutions that are related, yet different from that found by (35), (the “clock” algorithm). Moreover, transformers can exhibit sharp phase transitions (4) between these two solutions depending on width and attention strength. These results arise under specific settings of optimization and data starvation, discussed in more detail in (29). Specific to feature learning and lazy-versus-rich characterizations, (28) interpret grokking as a transition from a lazy, kernel-like regime to a rich regime of feature learning. Using readout scale as a proxy for laziness, they show higher readout scales delay generalization in several settings. However, for transformers solving modular addition, varying learning rate with readout scale $\alpha$ as done in (13) does not allow for comparable update dynamics across $\alpha$ in adaptive optimizers. We study this in *Section 3*, showing that effects of $\alpha$ can be confounded with those of hyper-parameters, hence showing limited applicability of the lazy-to-rich interpretation.
