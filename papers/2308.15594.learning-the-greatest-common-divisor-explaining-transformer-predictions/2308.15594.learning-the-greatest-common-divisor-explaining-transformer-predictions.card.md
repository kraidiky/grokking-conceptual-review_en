## Concepts covered

**[Grokking](../../concepts/grokking.md)** — the work gives the corpus a borderline case: a sudden jump of performance after a long shelf of the training loss, obtained where overfitting cannot occur by construction. A representative run at base $1000$: $13$ correct GCDs by epoch $84$, then "the training loss is flat for $100$ epochs, and the model seems to learn nothing more", and at epoch $188$ the GCD $3$ appears at an accuracy of $0.2\%$ and by epoch $193$ reaches $93\%$ — even though over those five epochs the model has seen only about 100 000 pairs with that GCD ([p. 4, para. 4](#p4-4)). The author's relation to grokking is cautiously formulated — "This phenomenon is akin to grokking (Power et al. 2022)" ([p. 4, para. 6](#p4-6)) — and in [section 7](#sec-7) he asks under a separate heading whether this is grokking and, by the definition of Power et al., answers in the negative: the data is generated on the fly from a space of $10^{12}$ pairs, "no overfitting can happen", and exactly one thing is named as shared with grokking — "a sudden change of accuracy after a long stagnation of the training loss" ([p. 9, para. 9](#p9-9)). What the work does not contain: no weight decay and no regularisation whatever, no fixed training set, no gap between training and validation error, and no passage from memorisation to generalisation — and the very word "grokked" in the list of contributions is placed in quotation marks. What is valuable for the corpus lies elsewhere: the content of the jump is here named by name — at the point of the jump a divisibility test by a new prime appears, and it is known exactly which predictions it changes ([fig. 5](#fig-5), [p. 9, para. 5](#p9-5)).

**[The memorisation phase / the plateau](../../concepts/memorization-phase.md)** — there is a plateau in the work, but there is nothing to memorise on it: there is no training set of fixed size at all, each epoch takes a fresh 300 000 examples ([p. 2, para. 5](#p2-5)), and the size of the task space is declared a guarantee of minimal duplication between the training and test sets ([p. 2, para. 7](#p2-7)). The plateau here is of two kinds: a flat training loss over the $100$ epochs before the jump ([p. 4, para. 4](#p4-4)) and — under uniform outcomes — a flat loss over the whole run, even while the number of correct GCDs rises in steps throughout ([p. 7, para. 2](#p7-2), [fig. 3](#fig-3)). The author calls these plateaux neither a memorisation phase nor overfitting, and the corpus's reading of the plateau cannot be carried over to them: for the second kind it is shown outright that what stands behind it is a sweep through the representatives of a class rather than the learning of a table by heart.

**[Phase transition](../../concepts/phase-transition.md)** — the step-like learning curves are the principal object observed here: "The learning curves have a step-like shape (figure 1), and GCDs are learnt in sudden batches" ([p. 4, para. 1](#p4-1), [fig. 1](#fig-1)), and at large bases the steps are described as "long eras of stagnation followed by sudden collapses of the loss and rises of the accuracy" ([p. 4, para. 6](#p4-6), [fig. 5](#fig-5)). It matters for the corpus that the mechanism of the steps is not postulated here but computed from the arithmetic structure of the task: every time a new divisor $p$ is learnt, all the existing classes split into multiples and non-multiples of $p$, and this gives a step ([p. 9, para. 5](#p9-5)). What the work does not contain: the language of phase transitions, order parameters, critical exponents or the word "transition" in the physical sense at all; the steps are described as an observed shape of the curve, and the author draws no analogy with statistical physics.

**[Emergence / emergent abilities](../../concepts/emergence.md)** — a rare case in the corpus in which the content of a suddenly appearing ability is established by name and without recourse to the weights: the ability is a divisibility test by a new prime, and after its appearance exactly those GCDs are predicted correctly all of whose prime powers have already been learnt ([p. 9, para. 4](#p9-4), [p. 9, para. 5](#p9-5)). The control quantity here is training time rather than scale: appendix B shows that the performance is stable from $300$ thousand to $700$ million parameters ([p. 13, para. 1](#p13-1)), that is, there is no threshold in model size in this task. What the work does not contain: the words "emergent" and "emergence" — the author does not use them, and the framing of emergent abilities cannot be credited to him.

**[When regularisation is necessary](../../concepts/regularization-necessity.md)** — the work is useful to the corpus as a negative data point: neither [weight decay](../../concepts/weight-decay.md), nor dropout, nor any other explicit regularisation is mentioned in it even once — the whole setting is exhausted by a four-layer transformer, Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-5}$ with no schedule and batches of $256$ ([p. 2, para. 5](#p2-5)) — and the steps and late jumps are observed all the same ([p. 4, para. 6](#p4-6)). A caveat is obligatory: this is evidence not of grokking without regularisation but of sudden jumps without it, since the author himself denies that the definition of Power et al. is satisfied here ([p. 9, para. 9](#p9-9)). What the work does not contain: an experiment in which regularisation is switched on and off — no comparison with a regularised run was carried out, and the author makes no claim that weight decay is unnecessary.

**[Mechanistic interpretability](../../concepts/mechanistic-interpretability.md)** — the work offers the corpus an alternative by construction: "Instead of looking at the model's parameters, I construct experiments that lay bare the algorithms the model executes" ([p. 9, para. 3](#p9-3)), and the preceding works on the explainability of arithmetic transformers are named as precisely those that "attempt to interpret the model weights (Nanda et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2023)" ([p. 2, para. 3](#p2-3)). The outcome is a complete description of the behaviour by three rules and a check of that description by a number: from the rules in [appendix C](#sec-c) a theoretical accuracy is derived and checked against the measured one across $20$ bases ([p. 14, para. 1](#p14-1)). Equally substantive is the limit: under uniform outcomes the explainability breaks down — "The model's predictions are no longer explainable, and the three rules are not observed" ([p. 8, para. 5](#p8-5)) — and this is carried into the abstract ([abstract](#p1-2)). What the work does not contain: not a single look inside the network — no weights, no embeddings, no attention heads, no ablations; the work isolates no circuits and does not use the word "circuit".

**[The canonical grokking proving ground](../../concepts/canonical-grokking-testbed.md)** — the setting of Power et al. is inherited here in no part, and this is worth keeping in mind when carrying conclusions over. The task is framed as a translation: a pair of integers written in digits in base $B$ is translated into the writing of their GCD ([p. 2, para. 4](#p2-4)); the symbols are informative (they are digits, not featureless tokens), and the fraction of the table used in training does not serve as a control quantity, because there is no table and the data is generated on the fly ([p. 2, para. 5](#p2-5)). Exactly one word is borrowed from Power et al., and it is placed in quotation marks. What the task gives the corpus in its own right: the chief control quantity here turns out to be the base $B$ of the writing, and the second the distribution of the training examples, and both these knobs are absent from the canonical proving ground.

**[The data fraction / the critical dataset size](../../concepts/data-fraction-critical-dataset-size.md)** — the work stands on the other side of this notion: there is no training set of fixed size, and there can be no threshold in the volume of data in it ([p. 2, para. 5](#p2-5), [p. 2, para. 7](#p2-7)). In place of volume the lever is the **shape** of the training distribution: log-uniform operands raise the accuracy from "$61$ and $97\%$" to "$94$ and $99\%$" ([p. 6, para. 3](#p6-3)), log-uniform outcomes bring the number of correct GCDs to $87$–$91$ ([p. 7, para. 1](#p7-1)), and a perfectly balanced, uniform distribution of outcomes worsens the performance and breaks the explainability ([p. 9, para. 8](#p9-8)). What the work does not contain: no quantity $D_{\text{crit}}$, no data fraction and no dependence of the time to the jump on the sample size — this axis the work does not touch at all.

**[Catastrophic forgetting](../../concepts/catastrophic-forgetting.md)** — there is one mention, but a precise one: a log-uniform distribution of operands "is akin to curriculum learning, but avoids catastrophic forgetting, because the training distribution never changes" ([p. 9, para. 8](#p9-8)). This is an argument rather than a measurement: there is no experiment with a changing curriculum in the work in which forgetting would be shown, and it computes no measure of forgetting.

## Points we could not reconcile

**For base $1000$ under uniform operands and outcomes, both $95$ correct GCDs and $71$ are named.** Section 6 reports: "By epoch 740, $95$ GCDs up to $100$ are correctly predicted" ([p. 9, para. 1](#p9-1)); appendix D.3 repeats the same number for epoch $800$ ([p. 15, para. 3](#p15-3)); and section 7 carries it into the conclusion — "Models trained on uniform outcomes predict $95$ GCDs" ([p. 9, para. 2](#p9-2)). Table 16, where the same base in the same setting is trained for $1200$ epochs, gives $71$ ([p. 16, para. 4](#p16-4)). Neither the repetition of the experiment nor the divergence is anywhere stipulated; when citing the number $95$ one should state that it comes from a single run of section 6 rather than from the summary table. Separately: figure 4, which depicts that very run, shows about $92$ correct GCDs at epoch $800$ rather than $95$, and is drawn as a single curve with no number of seeds given ([fig. 4](#fig-4)).

**"Only $27$ GCDs are predicted incorrectly" holds for one base out of three.** The claim refers to the bases $1024$, $2401$ and $2744$, and the enumeration that follows it does indeed give exactly $27$ ([p. 6, para. 4](#p6-4)). But table 6 credits those bases with $71$, $73$ and $72$ correct GCDs, that is, $29$, $27$ and $28$ incorrect: the enumeration agrees only for $2401$.

**The step-likeness of the curves under log-uniform operands is asserted but not shown.** The text says: "The learning curves retain their step-like shape, but become noisier and smoother" ([p. 6, para. 7](#p6-7)). In figures 6 and 7 — the very ones the sentence refers to — both the number of correct GCDs and the training loss run almost smoothly: there are no steps there comparable with those of figure 5 ([fig. 6](#fig-6), [fig. 7](#fig-7)). The hedge "smoother" does stand in the original, but "retain their step-like shape" is not confirmed by the figures.

**Repeated rows with different outcomes.** In table 4 the base $2401=7^{4}$ occupies two rows with different results ($10$ and $14$ GCDs, a different order of learning the non-divisors) ([p. 4, para. 6](#p4-6)); in table 5 the base $10000$ stands twice ($40$ and $62$ GCDs) ([p. 5, para. 3](#p5-3)). Neither the repetition nor the divergence is stipulated; whether these are two runs, two settings or a typo is unknown.

**Minor slips left in the published version.** A frequency of $200\%$ in table 8 (base $10$, epoch $268$, GCD $6$) ([p. 7, para. 3](#p7-3)); a value of $81.4$ among the $91.x$ in table 11 (base $12$, architecture $1/12$) ([p. 12, para. 10](#p12-10)); "$124$ dimensions" instead of 1024 in D.5 ([p. 17, para. 2](#p17-2)); and a broken sentence, "While the model In the course of training, as the model learns a new divisor…", in section 5 ([p. 6, para. 7](#p6-7)). In the same place, in sections 3–4, "$25$ GCDs" in adjacent paragraphs means now the value of the GCD ([p. 4, para. 1](#p4-1)) and now their number ([p. 4, para. 2](#p4-2)). All of this is carried into the translation as it stands.

## What is shown, what is conjectured and what is untested

**Shown and checked by a number.** The three rules of behaviour are not only formulated but checked in two independent ways: by name — tables 22–23 give the model's prediction for every GCD from $1$ to $100$ across six bases — and numerically: from the rules in [appendix C](#sec-c) a theoretical accuracy is derived and checked against the measured one across $20$ bases ([p. 14, para. 1](#p14-1)). This is a situation rare in the corpus, in which an explanation predicts a number rather than restating it. To the same heading belongs the analysis of uniform outcomes: the rules U1–U3 do not merely describe the chaos of the predictions but explain the size of the accuracy jumps, since $61\%$ of the examples of the natural set have GCD $1$ ([p. 8, para. 1](#p8-1)).

**Declared an argument by the author himself.** The final reading through a sieve is introduced by the words "The experiments indicate that…" ([p. 9, para. 4](#p9-4)); the necessity of an unbalanced distribution by the words "suggest"; and the carry-over to other tasks is marked three times as a possibility: "may extend to other arithmetic tasks", "These observations may extend to other arithmetic tasks", "This is a promising direction for future research" ([p. 9, para. 3](#p9-3), [p. 9, para. 8](#p9-8)).

**The only check of the carry-over is negative, and it appears neither in the abstract nor in the discussion.** [Appendix A](#sec-a) trains transformers on five operations over rational numbers: comparison is learnt almost perfectly, integer division in part, and all three tasks requiring the computation of a GCD (reducing a fraction, adding and multiplying fractions) are not learnt: the reduction gives no more than $0.21\%$, and the addition and multiplication exactly $0$ at both $M$ ([p. 12, para. 7](#p12-7)). This is the direct price of the claim "transformers learn to compute the GCD": the ability to predict the GCD of a pair does not carry over even to the nearest task in which the GCD serves as an intermediate step.

**The seed spread is given nowhere except in the figures.** All the tables give the "best of 3" or "best of 6" model with no means and no spreads. The only place where the variability is visible is figures 3 and 5–7, and it is large there: in figure 5, of the three runs at base $2023$ only one reaches $16$ GCDs (by epoch $\approx590$), while the other two stand at $11$ at epoch $800$ ([fig. 5](#fig-5)). Table 4 gives precisely $16$ for $2023$ as the "best model of 3": the spread between seeds is of the same order as the increment under analysis ([p. 4, para. 6](#p4-6)).

**Three usual explanations are removed.** Model size decides nothing — from $300$ thousand to $700$ million parameters the accuracy is the same ([p. 13, para. 1](#p13-1)); the batch size "appears to have little effect on performance"; and the phenomenon is not peculiar to transformers — LSTMs and GRUs give the same picture, though worse in magnitude ([p. 17, para. 2](#p17-2)).

## Common misreadings and overstatements

These are the card editor's remarks, not the authors' text: places where this paper restates someone else's results with an error, or without the qualifications their authors attached. The "details" links in the "Common misreadings and overstatements of this paper" sections of the cited works' cards lead here.

###### mc-2201-02177

**Power et al. (2201.02177) — "the classical pattern of grokking" retold with a falling training accuracy.** Analysing whether what he has before him is grokking, the author writes: `"No overfitting can happen, and the classical pattern of grokking, train accuracy dropping, and validation accuracy catching up after a long time, will not occur."` ([p. 9, para. 9](#p9-9)). In Power et al. the training accuracy does not fall: in the canonical figure it [becomes near perfect within $<10^{3}$ steps and stays there](../2201.02177.grokking-generalization-beyond-overfitting-on-small-algorithmic-datasets/2201.02177.grokking-generalization-beyond-overfitting-on-small-algorithmic-datasets.card.md#fig-1), and the validation accuracy "catches up" with it after $\sim10^{6}$ steps — the pattern consists in a *gap* between the two curves rather than in a fall of the upper one. The definition itself, which Charton cites correctly ("generalization far after overfitting"), contains no fall: [in Power et al. the subject is generalisation far beyond the point of overfitting](../2201.02177.grokking-generalization-beyond-overfitting-on-small-algorithmic-datasets/2201.02177.grokking-generalization-beyond-overfitting-on-small-algorithmic-datasets.card.md#p1-4). The error does not affect Charton's own conclusion — with data generated on the fly neither a gap nor a fall would arise — but a formulation taken from this paragraph credits the primary source with a picture it does not contain.

## Common misreadings and overstatements of this paper

Below are the patterns observed in the citing works of the corpus. For each it is said what exactly is distorted, with references to the author's paragraphs where the lost caveat stands. The "Erroneous citations" sections on the side of the citing cards have not yet been created for this work, so the detailed analyses are not yet addressed and the citing works are listed by simple links to their cards.

**"Grokking was observed on the GCD task" (widening).** The commonest way of citing this work is to place it in a list of settings in which grokking has been observed. Lyu et al. 2024 write `"grokking has been reported in learning group operations (Chughtai et al. 2023), learning sparse parity (Barak et al. 2022; Bhattamishra et al. 2023), learning greatest common divisor (Charton 2024), and image classification"`; Mohamadi et al. 2024, `"grokking can happen in tasks beyond modular arithmetic: in learning sparse parities [5, 6], image classifiers [27], greatest common divisors [9]"`; Notsawo et al. 2025, `"Charton (2024) on greatest common divisor (with Transformer)"`; Xu et al. 2026, `"grokking has also been observed in other learning paradigms, which include learning sparse parities (Barak et al., 2022; Merrill et al., 2023), learning the greatest common divisor of integers (Charton, 2024)"`; the same form is given by three works that have no cards as yet. A whole section is lost in the process: the author poses the question "Is this really grokking?" under a separate heading and, by the definition of Power et al., answers in the negative — the data is generated on the fly, "no overfitting can happen", and exactly one thing is named as shared with grokking, "a sudden change of accuracy after a long stagnation of the training loss" ([p. 9, para. 9](#p9-9)); in the observation itself the relation to grokking is expressed by the word "akin" ([p. 4, para. 6](#p4-6)), and in the list of contributions "grokked" stands in quotation marks. An exemplary formulation for comparison exists in the same corpus: Jeffares and van der Schaar 2025 cite the work not as an observation of grokking but as a source of a criterion — how far an effect is "grokking-like" depends among other things on whether the training performance reaches near perfection, `"near-perfect train performance rather than stagnation at some arbitrary level (Charton 2024)"`. Works concerned: [Lyu et al. 2024](../2311.18817.dichotomy-of-early-and-late-phase-implicit-biases-can-provably-induce-grokking/2311.18817.dichotomy-of-early-and-late-phase-implicit-biases-can-provably-induce-grokking.card.md), [Mohamadi et al. 2024](../2407.12332.why-do-you-grok-a-theoretical-analysis-of-grokking-modular-addition/2407.12332.why-do-you-grok-a-theoretical-analysis-of-grokking-modular-addition.card.md), [Notsawo et al. 2025](../2506.05718.grokking-beyond-the-euclidean-norm-of-model-parameters/2506.05718.grokking-beyond-the-euclidean-norm-of-model-parameters.card.md), [Xu et al. 2026](../2601.19791.to-grok-grokking-provable-grokking-in-ridge-regression/2601.19791.to-grok-grokking-provable-grokking-in-ridge-regression.card.md); no cards as yet for 2405.16658 (Park, Kim, Kim), 2412.10898 (Hu, Zhou, Yu) and 2605.14659 (So, Lee, No), which cite in the same form, nor for 2506.23286 (Jeffares, van der Schaar) with the exemplary formulation.

**"Models are able to compute the GCD" (widening).** Saxena et al. 2024 list the GCD among the complex mathematical tasks that machine-learning models are able to solve — `"ML models can learn other complex math tasks such as symbolic regression, linear algebra, discovering Lyapunov functions, computing Gröbner bases, polynomial simplification"`, and further in the same list the computation of the greatest common divisor, with a reference to this work among others. The formulation is true only under a suitable training distribution and falls apart without it: under uniform operands the model at base $10$ correctly predicts $13$ GCDs of the first hundred at an accuracy of $84.7\%$, and the author himself builds on this a warning about "the limits of naive, benchmark-based evaluations of arithmetic tasks": an accuracy of $95\%$ is attainable by a model predicting a handful of GCDs ([p. 9, para. 2](#p9-2)). Stronger still, the work's only check of the carry-over is negative: the three tasks in which the GCD serves as an intermediate step — reducing a fraction, adding and multiplying fractions — are not learnt at all ([p. 12, para. 7](#p12-7)). Work concerned: [Saxena et al. 2024](../2410.03569.making-hard-problems-easier-with-custom-data-distributions-and-loss-regularization/2410.03569.making-hard-problems-easier-with-custom-data-distributions-and-loss-regularization.card.md) (Charton himself is a co-author, so this is a self-citation).

**"The setting is as in Charton 2024" at different hyperparameters (erroneous).** A work on modular exponentiation reproduces this paper's frame and declares: `"For comparability with Charton 2024 train four-layer encoder-decoder transformers with embedding dimension 256, eight attention heads, batch size 256, and learning rate $10^{-4}$ with the Adam optimizer"`. Only the number of layers, the number of heads and the batch size coincide: in Charton's GCD experiments there are $512$ dimensions and a learning rate of $10^{-5}$ with no schedule ([p. 2, para. 5](#p2-5)); $10^{-4}$ with an inverse-square-root schedule and a warm-up belongs to a different setting — appendix A on the arithmetic of rational numbers, where there are also $512$ dimensions. A model of $256$ dimensions appears in the paper in exactly one place — in a column of table 10, among the single-layer models of the scaling experiment ([p. 12, para. 9](#p12-9)) — and has nothing to do with the experiments with which comparability is declared. The same work's other references to Charton (encoding integers as base digits, the analysis of deterministic prediction errors, the role of the sampling distribution) are accurate — the warning concerns one sentence about comparability rather than the work as a whole. Work concerned: 2506.23679 (no card as yet).
