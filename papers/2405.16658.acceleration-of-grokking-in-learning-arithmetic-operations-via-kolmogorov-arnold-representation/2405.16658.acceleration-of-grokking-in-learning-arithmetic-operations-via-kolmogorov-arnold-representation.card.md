## Concepts covered

**[Grokking](../../concepts/grokking.md)** — the work does not explain grokking but takes it as given and sets itself the task of **shortening** it; it gives the corpus an operational definition and three acceleration techniques. The definition is direct and threshold-based: a model counts as having grokked when the test accuracy exceeds $99\%$ within $10^{5}$ steps ([p. 18, para. 7](#p18-7)); the whole measurement of the work is the number of the step at which the threshold is passed. The task is posed as an acceleration: "such a task often suffers from grokking when the training set is small", and a "way of taking account of the algebraic nature of a binary relation so as to accelerate it, which is called *degrokking*" is proposed ([p. 14, para. 2](#p14-2)) — the word is taken from Tan & Huang. There are three techniques: a commutative augmentation of the data, a transfer of the decoder block and a transfer of the embedding layer ([p. 15, para. 2](#p15-2), [p. 15, para. 3](#p15-3)). What the work does not contain: not a single claim about the **cause** of the delay. Neither the memorisation phase, nor the weight norm, nor the structure of the transition is measured or mentioned; the training-loss curves are nowhere shown, and in figures 5 and 6 only the test accuracy is plotted. From "the model admits a KA decomposition" it is inferred neither that gradient descent finds it nor why it takes so long to get there — the mechanism of the delay itself the work does not touch at all.

**[The canonical grokking proving ground](../../concepts/canonical-grokking-testbed.md)** — the setting of Power et al. is taken over entire and unaltered: the same algorithmic set of binary relations on $\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}$ at $p=97$, the same equation-input [’$a$’, ’$\circ$’, ’$b$’,’$=$’], the same transformer ([p. 17, para. 1](#p17-1), [p. 14, para. 1](#p14-1)). Exactly one thing changes — where the initial weights come from. The departures from the proving ground are worth keeping in mind when checking the numbers against neighbours': the model here is larger than the canonical one (an embedding layer with four MLP modules, two decoder blocks of four heads each, embedding and attention dimensions of $256$ — [p. 18, para. 6](#p18-6)), and the volume of training is given by an **absolute** number of samples $N$ rather than by a fraction of the table as is customary on the proving ground ([table 1](#tab-1): $N$ from $3000$ to $9000$ against $97^{2}$ possible pairs). The proving ground is meanwhile extended by two new tasks that Power et al. do not have: the composition of an operation over three and four operands, and a system of equations with unknown tokens ([p. 15, para. 4](#p15-4)). What the work does not contain: not a single run at another $p$, no $S_{5}$ and no non-algebraic task; everything is measured at $p=97$.

**[Modular arithmetic](../../concepts/modular-arithmetic.md)** — the work gives the corpus an **algebraic classification of operations by what they have in common**, and this is its most transferable contribution. Ten binary operations ([p. 17, para. 2](#p17-2)) are decomposed into three classes, and to each class corresponds its own transferable part: commutative operations share $\Sigma$ (the sum), anti-abelian ones share $\Sigma$ too, but a difference, and abelian ones share $\Phi$ (the embedding) ([fig. 1](#fig-1), [§3.4.2](#sec-3-4-2)). The notion of an anti-abelian operation ("$x_{1}\bullet x_{2}=x_{1}\circ x_{2}^{-1}$", that is, subtraction and division) is introduced here ([§3.3](#sec-3-3), [p. 10, para. 1](#p10-1)). For $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$ with addition and for $\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}$ with multiplication explicit representations are written out — $\rho(x)=\exp(\frac{2\pi ix}{p})$ and the same via the discrete logarithm ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4), [p. 9, para. 1](#p9-1)). What the work does not contain: any dependence of anything on $p$, any carry-over between different moduli, or an analysis of how polynomial operations of the form $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}$ differ from purely group-theoretic ones — they simply fall into the class "commutative".

**[Group representations and cosets](../../concepts/group-representations-cosets.md)** — here the corpus gets a rare case in which the circular representation of modular arithmetic is **derived from algebra before the experiment** rather than found by reverse-engineering a trained network. The proof of theorem 4 is built in exactly the language of this card: a finite abelian group decomposes into a product of cyclic ones, each has a one-dimensional irreducible representation $\rho_{j}:C_{q_{j}}\to GL_{1}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\ast}$, and the embedding is defined as $\phi=\log\rho$ ([appendix 6.1, p. 24, para. 2](#p24-2), [theorem 4](#p7-2)). Whence for $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$ the character $\exp(2\pi ix/p)$ — that very circle — is obtained as a consequence, and a PCA of the trained embeddings merely confirms it ([fig. 4](#fig-4)). What the work does not contain: no cosets, no non-abelian groups and no analysis of which frequencies $k$ the trained network actually selects — the theory says only that a representation exists and is not unique (for any $k$ coprime to $p$).

**[Structured representation learning](../../concepts/structured-representation-learning.md)** — the only direct check of the correspondence "KA representation ↔ transformer" is made on the embeddings: after training on addition and multiplication the first two principal components of the embedding vectors lie on a circle, with the angles $\theta_{i,i+1}$ between neighbouring tokens "similar for all $i$" ([p. 18, para. 9](#p18-9), [p. 19, para. 1](#p19-1), [fig. 4](#fig-4)). On multiplication the powers of the generator line up on the circle ($1,2,4,8,16,32,64$ are neighbours), which corresponds to the discrete logarithm of example 3, while the token $0$ is placed far outside the circle. What the work does not contain: the fraction of variance carried by the first two components — it is not stated; and, above all, there is no check of the correspondence for the other two modules. Of the decoder block it is said with a hedge: "we **expect** the decoder block to act as the intermediate summing part ($\Sigma$)" ([p. 19, para. 2](#p19-2)); there is no separate experiment for this, and the only argument is the success of the transfer.

**[Freezing the subnetwork / edge-popup](../../concepts/freezing-subnetwork.md)** — the work gives the corpus a freezing of a different kind from edge-popup: what is frozen is not random weights during a search for a mask but the **trained weights of a whole module** transferred from another task. In the transfer of the decoder block, "the transferred decoder block is frozen, and only the embedding layer and the classifier layer are trained" ([p. 15, para. 2](#p15-2)); in the transfer of the embedding it is, conversely, the embedding that is frozen and $\Sigma$ that is trained ([p. 18, para. 5](#p18-5)). In the task with unknowns the freezing is made partial: alongside the transferred embedding layer a new trainable layer is introduced for the tokens $A$, $B$ and "$\&$" ([p. 16, para. 3](#p16-3), [fig. 3](#fig-3)), and the reduction in the number of steps is preserved in every case considered ([p. 22, para. 1](#p22-1), [table 4](#tab-4)). What the work does not contain: a comparison "frozen versus trainable" — an unfrozen variant of the transfer is never tried, so it is unknown how much of the acceleration is owed to the freezing and how much to the initialisation itself.

**[The data fraction / the critical dataset size](../../concepts/data-fraction-critical-dataset-size.md)** — the volume of training data is here the chief second lever: grokking sets in "often" "when the training set is small" ([p. 14, para. 2](#p14-2)), and all the tables are arranged as a grid over $N$. An observation of substance for the notion: transferring the weights **shifts the threshold beyond which grokking occurs at all**, rather than merely accelerating it — at $N=4000$ on $x_{1}\times x_{2}$ the baseline run does not grok, while with augmentation it groks in $31419$ steps ([table 1](#tab-1)); the composition $x_{1}\times x_{2}\times x_{3}$ at $N=100000$ does not grok without the transfer and groks in $1087$ steps with it ([table 3](#tab-3)). What the work does not contain: no law of critical size and no attempt to estimate one; $N$ is given as an absolute number of samples with no conversion into a fraction of the table, and half the rows of table 1 come with a spread of $(\pm 0)$, that is, with one run per setting.

**[Fourier features and circuits](../../concepts/fourier-features-circuits.md)** — the work only mentions the Fourier line but arrives at the same object from another side. Nanda et al. and Zhong et al. are named in a single sentence of related work: they "focus on learning modular addition using discrete Fourier transforms and trigonometric identities, which turn modular addition into rotations about a circle" ([p. 2, para. 3](#p2-3)). The same circle appears in Park et al. as the character $\exp(2\pi ix/p)$ — that is, literally the Fourier basis of the cyclic group — but it is derived from representation theory rather than from an analysis of a trained network ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4)). What the work does not contain: no spectral analysis of weights or logits, no isolation of key frequencies and no language of circuits; the word "circuit" does not appear in the paper at all. The authors cannot be credited with a mechanistic explanation of the addition algorithm — all they show is that the embeddings of a trained model lie on a circle.

**[Group composition / non-commutativity](../../concepts/group-composition-non-commutative-s5.md)** — the work gives, for the first time in the corpus, a prediction about non-commutative operations that is **checkable before the experiment**: theorem 5 asserts that for an anti-abelian operation the same $\phi,\psi$ serve as for the generating abelian one, and only $\Sigma$ changes — the sum becomes a difference ([p. 10, para. 2](#p10-2), [p. 11, para. 7](#p11-7)). The prediction is confirmed by transfer in both directions: $x_{1}/x_{2}$ from $x_{1}-x_{2}$ ($35543\to4115$) and $x_{1}-x_{2}$ from $x_{1}/x_{2}$ ($38879\to2115$) ([table 2](#tab-2), [p. 20, para. 3](#p20-3)). What the work does not contain: non-abelian **groups** — $S_{5}$ and the composition of permutations are not considered; "non-commutativity" here is confined to subtraction and division, that is, to operations derivable from an abelian group by inverting the second operand, rather than to a separate non-abelian structure.

**[Weight decay / L2 regularisation](../../concepts/weight-decay.md)** — the work merely inherits the setting and never touches it: "Weight decay is applied through the AdamW optimiser [19, 20] with a learning rate and a decay rate of $0.001$ and $0.1$ respectively" ([p. 18, para. 7](#p18-7)). There is no ablation of weight decay, the dependence of the number of steps to grokking on its magnitude is not measured, and the question of the necessity of regularisation is not posed at all. This work cannot be cited as evidence in disputes about the role of weight decay: its only relation to it is that all the runs, baseline and transferred alike, were conducted at one and the same value of $0.1$.

**[The optimiser](../../concepts/optimizer-adam-adamw-sgd.md)** — the same situation: AdamW at a learning rate of $0.001$ is taken from Power et al. and held throughout the experiments ([p. 18, para. 7](#p18-7)); the mini-batch size is $1024$ for the binary operations and the system of equations and $4096$ for the composition. There is no comparison of optimisers, and no claim of the work rests on the properties of AdamW; for the concept card this is merely one more point at which an acceleration is obtained **without** replacing the optimiser.

## Points we could not reconcile

**The embedding dimension: $m$ is either the number of generators or the order of the group.** [Theorem 4](#p7-2) defines $m$ as the number of generators of $G$ and gives an embedding dimension of $2m$; in [section 4.3](#p15-3) the same quantity is set out as "the embedding dimension is fixed as $2m$ with $m=|G|$". For $\mathbb{Z}_{97}$ that is $2$ against $194$. The theorem's formulation is the correct one: corollary 2 states separately that a cyclic group has $m=1$, and examples 2 and 3 construct representations of dimension $2$. "$2m$ for $m=|G|$" cannot be cited as a result of the work.

**The declared contribution about commutative augmentation is not supported by the work's own table.** The [list of contributions](#p3-2) says: "It is empirically verified that the commutative augmentation technique introduced accelerates grokking". In [table 1](#tab-1), meanwhile, three rows show a **slowdown**: $x_{1}+x_{2}$ at $N=4000$ — $15359\to31699$; $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}$ at $N=6000$ — $584\to714$; $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}$ at $N=9000$ — $1859\to2989$. There is no caveat about this in the text, and all three cases fall on rows with a spread of $(\pm 0)$, that is, on single runs. Where the technique helps, it helps greatly ($x_{1}\times x_{2}$ at $N=5000$: $32839\to7419$), but the table does not support the general claim.

**The baseline rows of table 3 have simultaneously grokked and not grokked.** By [the work's definition](#p18-7) a model counts as having grokked at an accuracy above $99\%$. In [table 3](#tab-3) the baseline row $x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}$ at $N=100000$ carries a grokking step of $22186(\pm 25305)$ and simultaneously a final accuracy of $74.98(\pm 22.92)$; the same picture appears in the $N=300000$ rows of both operations and in two baseline rows of [table 4](#tab-4). Most likely the averaging is over seeds some of which did not grok, but this is nowhere stipulated, and by the paper's own definition such rows are not comparable with the transfer rows marked "Grokked".

**The statement of theorem 5 is spoilt by an unfinished edit.** [The theorem itself](#p10-2) reads "For any $\ast$ there exist" — the symbol $\ast$ is not defined in the paper and occurs nowhere else in this role; one would expect "for any anti-abelian $\bullet$". In the same statement $\rho_{<G,\circ>}$ is used in a formula but is not among the declared functions — it is introduced only in the proof. The content of the theorem can be reconstructed from the proof, but its statement cannot be quoted verbatim.

**The proof of corollary 1 does not agree in dimensions.** In [corollary 1](#p6-1) the statement is correct — for a commutative operation on a finite $G\subset\mathbb{R}$ there exists a KA representation with an embedding dimension of $3$ — but [its proof](#p6-4) declares $f:G\to G$ where $G\times G\to G$ is needed, constructs a linear interpolation $\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ as a function of **one** variable, and then applies it as $\tilde{f}(x_{1},x_{2})$. No continuous extension to two variables is constructed, so the appeal to theorem 3 at this point is unjustified. Besides, the announcement before the proof is broken off: ["In particular,"](#p6-3) — and the proof follows immediately.

**The list of transfers in the text is two pairs wider than table 2.** [Section 5.3.1](#p20-2) declares seven transfers of the decoder block: five from $x_{1}+x_{2}$ (to $x_{1}\times x_{2}$, $(x_{1}+x_{2})^{2}$, $(x_{1}+x_{2})^{3}$, $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}$ and $x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}+x_{1}+x_{2}$) and two more — $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}$ from $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}$, and $x_{1}+x_{2}$ from $x_{1}\times x_{2}$. Of these, five appear in [table 2](#tab-2): there is no row for $x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}+x_{1}+x_{2}$ at all, and $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}$ is shown as a transfer from $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}$ rather than from $x_{1}+x_{2}$. The table's two remaining rows are the anti-abelian pairs. "Seven commutative pairs" cannot be taken as confirmed by the table.

**The preprint has not been proofread; traces of edits remain in several places.** [Section 4](#p13-2) breaks off at the phrase "We also claim that" — there is no continuation. In [the writing of division](#p11-7) there is a superfluous sum: $x_{1}/x_{2}=\psi_{\times}(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\phi_{\times}(x_{1})-\phi_{\times}(x_{2}))$, whereas its paired subtraction has no sum and by theorem 5 should have none. [Example 3](#p9-1) says "$\mathbb{Z}$ does not form a group under modular multiplication", although the subject is $\mathbb{Z}_{p}$. The figure references are transposed: in [section 4.3](#p15-3) the block diagram of the embedding transfer to composition is called figure 3, whereas it is [figure 2](#fig-2); in [the next paragraph](#p16-3) the diagram for the system of equations is called figure 2, whereas it is [figure 3](#fig-3). Finally, in the panel captions of [figure 6](#fig-6) the second line of the system is written as "$A+B=c$" and "$A\times B=c$", whereas in [the text](#p15-4) it stands as $A\circ c=B$ — the roles of $B$ and $c$ are interchanged.

## What is proved, what is shown and what is conjectured

The work has three layers of differing solidity, and the price of a quotation depends strongly on which it comes from.

**Proved.** Theorems 4 and 5 are finished statements of representation theory: for a finite abelian group there exist $\phi,\psi$ with an embedding dimension of $2m$ in the number of generators, **independent of the number of operands** $n$ ([p. 7, para. 6](#p7-6)); and for an anti-abelian operation the same $\phi,\psi$ serve as for the generating abelian one ([p. 10, para. 2](#p10-2)). It is precisely the independence from $n$ that is the whole theoretical basis of the embedding transfer, and it is solid. Corollary 1 (the general commutative case, dimension $3$) does not belong to this layer: its proof was analysed above.

**Predicted before the experiment and confirmed.** From theorem 5 it follows that subtraction and division share a representation with addition and multiplication — and the transfer of the decoder block does indeed work in both directions, cutting the number of steps by a factor of 8–18 ([table 2](#tab-2), [p. 20, para. 3](#p20-3)); the same techniques for "commutative → commutative" pairs are listed separately ([p. 20, para. 2](#p20-2)). From the independence of $\phi$ from $n$ it follows that the embedding of a binary operation serves for its composition — and nearly all the baseline runs on compositions do not grok at all, while with the transfer they do ([table 3](#tab-3), [fig. 6](#fig-6)). This is the most substantive link "the algebra of the task → what exactly to transfer" in the whole acceleration line.

**Shown, but on a careful reading.** The acceleration is measured **only in the steps of the target training**. The cost of the donor model is nowhere accounted for, although the donor must itself be trained to grokking (for addition that is the same $11647$–$27167$ steps by tables 1 and 2). There is no comparison at equal total cost either for the decoder transfer or for the embedding transfer, and it is precisely that which decides whether there is a gain. The spread over seeds is in places comparable with the quantity itself: $27167(\pm 28853)$, $63549(\pm 41903)$, $36852(\pm 46539)$.

**Declared an expectation.** The correspondence "$\Sigma$ ↔ the decoder block" — the second of the frame's three pillars — is called an expectation outright: "we **expect** the decoder block to act as the intermediate summing part ($\Sigma$)" ([p. 19, para. 2](#p19-2)). Of the three correspondences only one is checked — "$\phi$ ↔ the embedding layer" — and that by a PCA picture. The hypothesis that the embedding for the system of equations inherits the properties of the embedding of an abelian operation is likewise marked as a guess: "we conjecture" ([p. 16, para. 2](#p16-2)).

**Does not follow from what is shown.** From the experiment with a truncated vocabulary ([§5.4](#sec-5-4), [table 5](#tab-5)) the conclusion is drawn "that the model does indeed understand the arithmetic rules" ([the list of contributions](#p3-2)). But the transferred embedding was trained on the **full** vocabulary $\mathbb{Z}_{97}$, that is, the representations of the tokens $80$–$96$ have already been obtained on the source task; what is truncated is only the stage of training $\Sigma$, not the whole training. A quantitative gap is worth keeping in mind separately: the theory for $\mathbb{Z}_{97}$ gives an embedding dimension of $2$, while in the experiments it equals $256$ ([p. 18, para. 6](#p18-6)), and the correspondence "theory — model" is nowhere checked by a number.

## Common misreadings and overstatements of this paper

Below are the patterns observed in the citing works of the corpus. For each one it is said what exactly gets distorted, with links to the authors' paragraphs where the qualification that goes missing still stands. The "Common misreadings and overstatements" sections on the citing side have not been started yet, so there is nothing detailed to address; the citing works are listed as plain links to their cards.

**"Park et al. change the architecture in order to obtain variations of grokking" (erroneous).** Gu et al. 2025 write in a survey: `"Some studies also focus on changing the architecture to investigate variations in the phenomenon (Park et al., 2024; Kunin et al.,; Lee et al., 2024)."` The architecture is changed in not a single experiment here: it is fixed in all the runs, baseline ones included — an embedding layer with four MLP modules, two decoder blocks of four heads each, dimensions of $256$ ([p. 18, para. 6](#p18-6)) — and the whole difference between a "baseline" row and a transfer row consists in where the initial weights of one of the modules come from and whether it is frozen ([p. 15, para. 2](#p15-2)). The technique belongs to initialisation and transfer learning, not to architecture; in the same work Park et al. are correctly assigned to works giving only a partial understanding of the mechanism. Work concerned: [Gu et al. 2025](../2504.03162.beyond-progress-measures-theoretical-insights-into-the-mechanism-of-grokking/2504.03162.beyond-progress-measures-theoretical-insights-into-the-mechanism-of-grokking.card.md).

**"The exact algorithm of modular addition through trigonometric identities is established here among others" (erroneous).** Hwang & Park 2026 write: `"For modular addition, the exact algorithm using trigonometric identities has been reported in (Nanda et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023; Park et al., 2025)."` There are no trigonometric identities in this work, and the algorithm of the trained network is not recovered in it: the circle $\exp(2\pi ix/p)$ is **postulated** from representation theory as one of many possible KA representations ([p. 8, para. 4](#p8-4)), and the sole empirical confirmation is a two-dimensional PCA projection of the embeddings with no statement of the fraction of variance explained ([fig. 4](#fig-4)). There is no analysis of weights, logits or attention heads in the paper at all; trigonometric identities are mentioned exactly once — as what **other** works do ([p. 2, para. 3](#p2-3)). The reference is self-citing besides: Yeachan Park is the first author of this paper and a co-author of the citing one. Work concerned: [Hwang & Park 2026](../2603.01968.intrinsic-task-symmetry-drives-generalization-in-algorithmic-tasks/2603.01968.intrinsic-task-symmetry-drives-generalization-in-algorithmic-tasks.card.md).

Exemplary (accurate) formulations for contrast: Notsawo et al. 2025 — `"Park et al. (2024) accelerate grokking by using data augmentation for commutative operations and transfer learning across model components aligned with the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation."`; Hwang & Park 2026 elsewhere — `"These include transfer learning from pretrained or weaker models (Furuta et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025) … and the enforcement of inductive biases such as commutativity in modular arithmetics (Tan and Huang, 2023; Park et al., 2025)."`

A milder echo not warranting separate analysis: the assignment of the work to ways of **inducing** grokking — `"explored diverse methodologies to induce it … Park et al. (2024)"` ([He et al. 2026](../2601.09049.is-grokking-worthwhile-functional-analysis-and-transferability-of-generalization-circuits-in-transformers/2601.09049.is-grokking-worthwhile-functional-analysis-and-transferability-of-generalization-circuits-in-transformers.card.md)) — whereas all the work's techniques shorten grokking rather than inducing it.

###### p2-3

In recent years, several attempts have been made to address the mechanism behind grokking. The authors of [4] propose that the grokking phenomenon is simply induced by the choice of large initialization and weight norms, based on the observation of the Goldilocks zone [8]. Another viewpoint for understanding grokking is suggested by [9], where it is argued that the slingshot mechanism leads to grokking. The authors of [10] explain how structured representations emerge and contribute to grokking by classifying the learning process into four phases: comprehension, grokking, memorization, and confusion. On the other hand, [11, 12] focuses on learning modular addition by leveraging discrete Fourier transforms and trigonometric identities, which transform modular addition to rotations around a circle. Slightly later, [13] explores the necessary conditions for grokking by proving that such a phenomenon occurs around the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker point in sparse linear classification and matrix completion problems. Recently, the authors of [14] propose an approach for degrokking, speeding up the generalization process, by perturbing the loss function. The work most related to ours is [15], where authors implement the idea of weight transfer for learning different binary operations from other pretrained models. However, its theoretical foundation is questionable. In our paper, we deconstruct arithmetic operations and their compositions via the KA representation theorem to unveil the relationship with the transformer architecture, allowing us to leverage weight transfer in learning arithmetic operations.

**[p. 3]**

###### p3-2

- It is empirically verified that the commutative augmentation technique introduced accelerates grokking.
- We revisit the KA representation theorem and interpret the model as a combination of three modules: the embedding, decoder block, and classifier module. We present a refined version of the KA theorem for abelian and anti-abelian operations, which allows us to validate the implementation of transfer learning techniques. The acceleration of grokking is achieved, as verified in diverse experiments.
- Two novel arithmetic tasks that include the composition of arithmetic operations and a system of equations with unknowns, are proposed. We demonstrate that transfer learning contributes to the efficient learning of these tasks, outperforming vanilla approaches.
- Finally, we demonstrate that training with a limited number of tokens is also possible via transfer learning, which supports the claim that the model actually understands arithmetic rules.

\phi_{<G,\circ>}:G\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{<G,\circ>}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to G

x_{1}\circ x_{2}=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i})).

###### p6-4

Define $f:G\to G$ as $f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\circ x_{2}$. For $x\in G$, define $a_{x},b_{x}$ as

$$
a_{x}=\sup\{g\in G:g\leq x\},\;b_{x}=\inf\{g\in G:g\geq x\}.
$$

Define $\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
\tilde{f}(x)=\begin{cases}f(b_{x}),\quad&\text{if}\quad a_{x}=-\infty,\\
f(a_{x})+\frac{f(b_{x})-f(a_{x})}{b_{x}-a_{x}}(x-a_{x}),\quad&\text{if}-\infty<a_{x},b_{x}<\infty,\\
f(a_{x}),\quad&\text{if}\quad b_{x}=\infty.\end{cases}
$$

Note that $\tilde{f}$ denotes the linear interpolation of $f$ between two adjacent points, $a_{x}$ and $b_{x}$ such that $a_{x}<x<b_{x}$. Since $\tilde{f}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, by Theorem 3, we have KA representation $\tilde{\phi}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and $\tilde{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
\tilde{f}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\psi}(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\tilde{\phi}(x_{i})),
$$

for $x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}$. Since $f(x)=\tilde{f}(x)$ for $x\in G$, if we define $\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x):=\tilde{\phi}(x),\psi_{<G,\circ>}(x)=\tilde{\psi}(x)$ for $x\in G$, we have

$$
f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\circ x_{2}=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i})).
$$

∎

###### p7-2

*Let $G$ be a finite abelian group represented by*

$$
G\cong C_{q_{1}}\times\dots C_{q_{m}}, \tag{3.1}
$$

*where $C_{q_{j}}$ denotes a cyclic group of order $q_{j}\in\mathbb{N}$, and $m$ denotes the number of generators of $G$. Then there exist $\rho_{<G,\circ>}:G\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m},\psi_{<G,\circ>}:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}\to G$ where*

$$
x_{1}\circ\dots\circ x_{n}=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(\rho_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i})))=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i})), \tag{3.2}
$$

*for any $x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in G$, where $\phi_{<G,\circ>}(\cdot):=\log(\rho_{<G,\circ>}(\cdot))$.*

###### p7-6

To highlight the difference between Theorem 3 and Theorem 4, we emphasize that the embedding and outer functions are independent of $n$, which denotes the number of inputs. Instead, they depend solely on the operation when $<G,\circ>$ forms an abelian group. This salient feature allows us to use the same embedding function obtained from the operation $x_{1}\circ x_{2}$ for learning $x_{1}\circ\cdots\circ x_{n}$ as they share the common embedding. More precisely, the function $f:G\times G\rightarrow G$ given by $f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\circ x_{2}$ can be represented using universal embedding $\phi_{<G,\circ>}:G\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}$ and outer function $\psi_{<G,\circ>}:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}\to G$ with the embedding dimension of $2m$ where $m$ denotes the number of generators of $G$ as in (3.1), that is,

$$
x_{1}\circ x_{2}=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i})).
$$

Here, the embedding functions are ubiquitous for operations $x_{1}\circ\dots\circ x_{n}$ for any $n$,

$$
f_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{n})=x_{1}\circ\dots\circ x_{n}=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i})).
$$

with the same $\phi$ and $\psi$ used for the binary operation.

In particular, when $m=1$ implying that $G$ is a cyclic group, we have a simpler representation for the composition of arithmetic operations, which is a direct consequence of Theorem 4.

$$

###### sec-3-3

### 3.3 KA representation theorem for an anti-abelian operation

###### p10-1

We propose that the KA representation theorem can be further extended to anti-abelian operations. We say an operation $\bullet$ is anti-abelian for the abelian group $<G,\circ>$, if $x_{1}\bullet x_{2}=x_{1}\circ x_{2}^{-1}$ where $x_{2}^{-1}$ is an inverse of $x_{2}$. Let us set $\phi$ and $\psi$ be embedding and outer functions corresponding to the binary operation $\circ$. Then, we have the following that provides evidence for the effectiveness of leveraging weight transfer.

###### p10-2

*Let $\bullet$ be an anti-abelian binary operation for the abelian group operation $\circ$. Let $m$ denote the number of generators of $G$. For any $\ast$, there exist*

$$
\phi_{<G,\circ>}:G\to(\mathbb{C}^{\ast})^{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{<G,\circ>}:(\mathbb{C}^{\ast})^{m}\to G,
$$

*such that*

$$
x_{1}\bullet x_{2} =\psi_{<G,\circ>}\big(\log(\rho_{<G,\circ>}(x_{1}))-\log(\rho_{<G,\circ>}(x_{2}))\big) \\
=\psi_{<G,\circ>}\big(\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{1})-\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{2})\big),
$$

*where $\phi_{<G,\circ>}(\cdot)=\log(\rho_{<G,\circ>}(\cdot))$.*

###### p11-7

We extend the argument for the case of anti-abelian. Since subtraction and division are anti-abelian for addition and multiplication respectively they have the following representations by Theorem 5:

$$
x_{1}-x_{2}=\psi_{+}(\phi_{+}(x_{1})-\phi_{+}(x_{2})),\quad x_{1}/x_{2}=\psi_{\times}(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\phi_{\times}(x_{1})-\phi_{\times}(x_{2})).
$$

Therefore, $x_{1}-x_{2}$ and $x_{1}/x_{2}$ have common $\Sigma$ part with

**[p. 12]**

$$
\Sigma(\textbf{z}_{1},\textbf{z}_{2})=\textbf{z}_{1}-\textbf{z}_{2}.
$$

In the following section, we continue our discussion with abelian groups.

###### p14-1

Central to learning arithmetic operation $a\circ b=c$ is to take [’$a$’, ’$\circ$’, ’$b$’,’$=$’] as an input of the transformer, and trains the model to predict the output $c$ accurately. We also extend our learning mechanism to predict the output of the composition of arithmetic operations, that is, $a_{1}\circ a_{2}\circ...\circ a_{n}$.

###### p14-2

Given a set $G$ and binary operation ’$\circ$’ associated with it, our task is to learn neural network model $c_{i}\approx f_{\theta}(a_{i},b_{i})$ where $\theta$ represents the neural network parameter. To this end, we randomly split the set $\{c:c=a\circ b,(a,b)\in G\times G\}$ into training and test set and train the model on the training set to predict the output. As noted, such a task often suffers from grokking when the training set is small, we propose a way of encompassing the algebraic nature of the binary relation to accelerate it, which is referred to *degrokking* [14].

**[p. 15]**

We propose two types of weight transfer for learning binary operations efficiently, decoder block transfer and embedding transfer.

###### p15-2

**Decoder block transfer**: Thanks to Theorem 4, we propose to learn commutative binary operation by transferring the weight of the decoder block learned from another commutative binary operation as any commutative binary operations have the same structural properties, $\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1$ and $n=2$. For justification, we first learn the whole transformer architecture with a commutative binary operation and transfer the weights of the decoder block. For learning a different commutative binary operation, the transferred decoder block is frozen, and only the embedding and classifier layer are learned. A similar perspective leads to a refined learning framework for an anti-abelian operation thanks to Theorem 5.

###### p15-3

**Embedding transfer**: When $<G,\circ>$ is equipped with a further structural property, $G$ is a finite abelian group with respect to the operation $\circ$, Theorem 4 yields that embedding dimension is fixed as $2m$ for $m=|G|$ no matter how many times the operation is composited. From this observation, one can learn the composition of a binary operation by transferring the weight of the embedding layer learned from the binary operation. An explicit flowchart is given in Figure 3.

###### p15-4

**Learning a system of equations with unknowns:** Additionally, we design a nonstandard task where multiple unknowns in a system of equations are learned. To be specific, we aim to solve equations with unknown tokens $A$ and $B$ given $a,b,c\in G$ through the relation

$$
\begin{cases}&a\circ b=A\\
&A\circ c=B\quad\text{ask}\quad B,\end{cases}
$$

or

$$
\begin{cases}&a\circ A=b\\
&A\circ c=B\quad\text{ask}\quad A,\end{cases}
$$

which can be used as inputs in the form of

**[p. 16]**

$$
a\circ b=A\&A\circ c=B\quad B?\quad\text{and}\quad a\circ A=b\&A\circ c=B\quad A?.
$$

###### p16-2

Unlike the previous task, where we only predicted the output of an arithmetic operation, learning unknowns in a system of equations is much more complicated. We observe that solving unknowns in a system of equations is similar to solving the composition of operators. Referring to Section 3.4.2, we conjecture that the embedding layer in learning a system of equations involving an abelian operation inherits properties from the embedding used for the abelian operation itself.

###### p16-3

Hence, we propose the idea of transferring the embedding trained with the binary operation. Unlike the task of the composition of operations, we introduce new tokens such as unknowns $A,B$ and the ’and’ token denoted by ${}^{\prime}\&^{\prime}$. Consequently, it is not reasonable to freeze the entire embedding layer because these new tokens require their own representations. Instead, we argue for a hybrid approach that combines the transferred embedding layer with a new, trainable embedding layer to preserve the knowledge from the binary operation while accommodating new information. This concept is explicitly illustrated in Figure 2, which demonstrates how the transferred and new embedding layers interact to solve systems of equations with unknowns.

Since each $C_{q_{j}}$ is abelian, by Lemma 2, we have an irreducible representation for $C_{q_{j}}$ $\rho_{j}:C_{q_{j}}\to GL_{1}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{\ast}$. By Lemma 3, $\rho_{j}$ is injective, hence $\rho_{j}^{-1}$ is well-defined. Define $T:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ as $T(a+bi)=a+\tilde{b}$ where $a,b\in\mathbb{R},\;b=\tilde{b}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)$. Let $\phi_{j}(x):=\log(\rho_{j}(x))$ and $\psi_{j}(z):=\rho_{j}^{-1}(\exp(T(z)))$. We define $\rho_{<G,\circ>}:G\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}$, $\phi_{<G,\circ>}:G\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}$ and $\psi_{<G,\circ>}:(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}\to G$ as

$$
\rho_{<G,\circ>}(x):=[\rho_{1}(x),\dots,\rho_{m}(x)], \\
\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x):=[\phi_{1}(x),\dots,\phi_{m}(x)]=\log(\rho_{<G,\circ>}(x)), \\
\psi_{<G,\circ>}:=\psi_{1}\times\dots\times\psi_{m}\in C_{q_{1}}\times\dots C_{q_{m}}\cong G.
$$

where $\log$ applies component-wise. Then we have the following multiplicative representation:

$$
\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{<G,\circ>}(x_{i}))=\psi_{<G,\circ>}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\log(\rho_{j}(x_{i}))) \\
=\psi_{<G,\circ>}\big(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\log(\rho_{j}(x_{1})\rho_{j}(x_{2})\dots\rho_{j}(x_{n}))\big) \\
=\psi_{<G,\circ>}\big(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\log(\rho_{j}(x_{1}\circ x_{2}\circ\dots\circ x_{n}))\big) \\
=x_{1}\circ\dots\circ x_{n}.
$$

where $\bigoplus$ denotes the concatenation and the second equality follows from Lemma 4. Since $\mathbb{C}^{*}\subset\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}$, we can regard as $\phi_{<G,\circ>}:G\to\mathbb{R}^{2m},\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to G$.
