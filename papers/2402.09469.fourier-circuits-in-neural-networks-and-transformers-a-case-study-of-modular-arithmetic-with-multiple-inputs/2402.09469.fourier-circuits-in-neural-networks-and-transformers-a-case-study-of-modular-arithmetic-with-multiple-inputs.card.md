# Li, Liang, Shi, Song, Zhou 2024 — Fourier Circuits in Neural Networks and Transformers: A Case Study of Modular Arithmetic with Multiple Inputs

## Concepts covered

**[Fourier features and circuits](../../concepts/fourier-features-circuits.md)** — the subject of the work. It is shown that in a network with one hidden layer and a polynomial activation every neuron of the maximum-margin solution is occupied by exactly **one** frequency: its weights have the form $\beta\cos(\theta^{*}+2\pi\zeta a/p)$ with a frequency $\zeta$ common to all the inputs and the output ([`p8-7`](#p8-7), [`p10-4`](#p10-4)). Moreover, every frequency from $\{1,\ldots,\frac{p-1}{2}\}$ is employed ([`p8-7`](#p8-7)).

**[Margin maximisation and implicit bias](../../concepts/margin-maximization-implicit-bias.md)** — the method of proof. The support is lemma 3.7: for homogeneous maps the normalised margin of the global minimisers of the smoothed loss converges to the maximum one as $\lambda\to 0$ ([`p8-3`](#p8-3)). Instead of a non-convex optimisation, then, the maximum-margin solution is analysed — and it is precisely that solution which turns out to be Fourier-sparse.

**[Modular arithmetic](../../concepts/modular-arithmetic.md)** — the task is extended from two inputs to $k$: $(a_{1}+\dots+a_{k})\bmod p$ ([`p4-5`](#p4-5)). The earlier theoretical work [58] analysed only two inputs ([`p4-4`](#p4-4)), whereas here a closed form of the maximum margin $\gamma^{*}=\frac{2(k!)}{(2k+2)^{(k+1)/2}(p-1)p^{(k-1)/2}}$ and a bound on the number of neurons $m\geq 2^{2k-2}\cdot(p-1)$ are obtained ([`p5-1`](#p5-1)).

**[Grokking](../../concepts/grokking.md)** — it is not explained here but **measured as a consequence**. As the number of inputs $k$ grows, grokking weakens, and this is predicted by the analysis: the complexity of the class of maps grows, more neurons are needed, and at a fixed $m$ the model leaves the lazy regime faster ([`p12-3`](#p12-3), [`fig-4`](#fig-4)). Hence the suggestion of taking theorem 4.1 as a measure of the complexity of the data.

**[Lazy and rich regimes](../../concepts/lazy-to-rich-kernel-to-feature-learning.md)** — the explanation of the weakening grokking leans on the works of Kumar et al. and Lyu et al.: the network is first in the kernel regime and then passes abruptly into a feature-learning one. The kernel regime requires many more neurons than the $\Omega(2^{2k})$ of the theorem, so at a large $k$ it simply does not arise — and the plateau before the grokking grows shorter ([`p12-4`](#p12-4)).

**[Mechanistic interpretability](../../concepts/mechanistic-interpretability.md)** — the work gives a theoretical foundation to what Nanda et al. found experimentally: trained transformers compute modular addition by rotations around a circle, leaning on the identity $\cos(2\pi\zeta(a_{1}+a_{2}-c)/p)$ ([`p4-3`](#p4-3)).

**[Attention and heads](../../concepts/attention-routing-heads.md)** — the same picture is found in a single-layer transformer too: the attention matrix $W^{KQ}$ takes on a two-dimensional cosine appearance, and its two-dimensional Fourier spectrum is single-component ([`p12-2`](#p12-2), [`fig-3`](#fig-3)). Hence the conclusion that the attention layer is trained by the same mechanism as a network with one hidden layer.

**[Sparse parity](../../concepts/sparse-parity.md)** — a direct connection: at $p=2$ the task degenerates into parity, for which the hardness of learning by statistical queries is known. The bound of $\Omega(\exp(k))$ neurons from theorem 4.1 agrees with the known lower bound of Barak et al. ([`p14-2`](#p14-2)).

**[Width and the number of neurons](../../concepts/data-fraction-critical-dataset-size.md)** — the work gives a sufficient condition on the width: $m\geq 2^{2k-1}\cdot\frac{p-1}{2}$ neurons suffice for the maximum-margin solution to be constructed ([`p8-7`](#p8-7)); the authors themselves caveat, meanwhile, that the bound may not be the tightest ([`p10-2`](#p10-2)).

**[Double descent](../../concepts/double-descent.md)** — in the discussion the work ties grokking to benign overfitting and implicit bias: grokking occurs if the early bias leads to an overfitted solution and the late one to a generalising solution ([`p14-1`](#p14-1)).

**[The emergence of features](../../concepts/feature-emergence-feature-learning.md)** — the experimental part: in the trained network the embeddings are single-component in frequency space (the largest normalised power is almost 1), and the distribution of the chosen frequencies across the neurons is nearly uniform ([`fig-2`](#fig-2), [`p11-7`](#p11-7)).

It is worth saying separately what the work does **not** contain. There is no analysis of the training dynamics: everything is proved about the maximum-margin solution rather than about how SGD arrives at it. Nor is there an explanation of grokking itself — the authors admit outright that the theorem gives an intuitive consideration but does not fully explain what is seen in figure 4 ([`p23-3`](#p23-3)). Finally, the activation is taken to be polynomial (for the sake of homogeneity and the convenience of the trigonometric derivations) rather than ReLU ([`p6-4`](#p6-4)).

## What is shown and what is conjectured

The card editor's remarks following the analysis of the claims (`…claims.txt`). The work is theoretical: 15 pages of main text and 39 pages of proofs.

**The claim is precise and checkable.** A closed form of the maximum margin at $k$ inputs is given, and it is shown that every neuron of the solution is occupied by one frequency while all the frequencies are covered ([`p8-7`](#p8-7)). This is not "the network learns something Fourier-like" but a statement with a number in it.

**The experimental check answers the theorem specifically rather than the general idea.** The network is trained with exactly the number of neurons the bound gives ($m=2944=2^{2k-2}\cdot(p-1)$ at $k=4$, $p=47$), and both consequences are checked separately: the single-component spectrum of each neuron and the covering of all the frequencies ([`p11-7`](#p11-7), [`fig-1`](#fig-1), [`fig-2`](#fig-2)).

**The carry-over to a transformer is performed and looks convincing.** The matrix $W^{KQ}$ of a single-layer transformer takes on a two-dimensional cosine appearance with a single-component two-dimensional spectrum ([`p12-2`](#p12-2)). The theory does not cover this, and the authors do not extend it to transformers — but the correspondence is shown at three values of $k$.

**The prediction about the weakening grokking is the most valuable thing for the corpus.** It is made **before** the experiment, derived from the number of neurons needed for the maximum-margin solution, and confirmed at $k=2,3,4,5$ ([`p12-3`](#p12-3), [`fig-4`](#fig-4)). Checkable predictions of this kind are not many in the corpus.

**The connection with the hardness of learning parity is drawn honestly.** At $p=2$ the task degenerates into parity, and the requirement of $\Omega(\exp(k))$ neurons agrees with the known lower bound ([`p14-2`](#p14-2)). This is an independent plausibility check of the estimate.

**What is proved, however, concerns the maximum margin rather than the course of the training.** The whole theory describes the solution $\theta^{*}$ to which the smoothed loss converges as $\lambda\to 0$ ([`p8-3`](#p8-3)); not one SGD step appears in the proofs. Hence the gap: grokking is a phenomenon of **dynamics**, and it is explained here by a property of the limiting point.

**The activation is chosen to suit the proof.** A polynomial activation is taken for the sake of homogeneity (needed for lemma 3.7) and the ease of the trigonometric derivations ([`p6-4`](#p6-4)). For ReLU networks, on which grokking is ordinarily observed, the result is not shown.

**The bound on the number of neurons is probably loose.** The authors themselves write that the lower bound on $m$ "may not be the tightest" ([`p10-2`](#p10-2)); at $k=5$ it requires $2^{9}\cdot 11=5632$ neurons, and the experiments are run at exactly that number — that is, sufficiency is checked, not necessity.

**The explanation of the weakening grokking remains an argument.** The chain "a larger $k$ → a more complex class of maps → no kernel regime → a shorter plateau" ([`p12-4`](#p12-4)) is plausible, but not one of its steps is measured: neither the kernel regime, nor its disappearance, nor the connection with the number of neurons. The authors admit this in the limitations section ([`p23-3`](#p23-3)).

**There are few runs.** All the results are obtained on 3 seeds ([`p48-3`](#p48-3) and appendix J.1), and for figures 1–3 no deviations are given at all; individual randomly chosen neurons and attention heads are shown.

**The literature survey is padded with references to the authors' own works.** The section on the Fourier transform in appendix C cites a dozen works with no bearing on grokking ([`p23-6`](#p23-6)); part of the bibliography consists of works by the same authors on entirely different subjects.

**Its place in the corpus.** The work is not about grokking but about **which solution** a network selects in the task on which grokking is studied. Three things are valuable: the proved single-frequency character of the neurons and the covering of all the frequencies at $k$ inputs; the closed form of the maximum margin with a bound on the width; and the prediction derived from that bound that grokking weakens as $k$ grows, confirmed by experiment. The price: the theory describes the limiting point rather than the road to it; the activation is polynomial rather than ReLU; and the phenomenon of grokking itself the work, by the authors' own admission, does not explain.
